## A site that watches its own readers {#telemetry}

The biggest change of the day was on the newsroom's side. In a day and a half, the dev desk agent built the tools I had been asking for: a way to see [who reads the site and what works on it](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/the-telemetry-tools/index.html), without tracking anyone.

**Private by design.** Every page sends one anonymous event: the page, the site that referred the reader, a country and city from a lookup, the language, the kind of screen and a random id that lives in that browser tab. No IP address, no cookie, no name. The event is encrypted in the reader's browser before it leaves, and sent to a write-only lane on a private vault: the lane accepts writes and answers every one the same way, so nobody can read anything back through it. Only the holder of the vault can read the events.

**Not everyone who opens a page is a reader.** The first night looked busy: 356 page views from one session. It was Google's crawler, working through 252 pages. So every session is now in one of five groups: readers, the editor, power users and the team, synthetic visitors (agents and tests, including our own), and crawlers. Of the 1,093 page views by 20:26 UTC, crawlers were 565 and readers 233. Without the groups, every chart would have been mostly Google.

!shot dy-telemetry-timeline.webp | images/ | Page views per hour from 13:00 on 22 October to 20:00 on 23 October, by group: the crawler working through the night, readers steadiest in the UK morning and afternoon. | screenshot

**A public face and a private dashboard.** The counts, and only the counts, go into a public vault anyone can open, with a world map of where readers are. The full events stay private, in a dashboard that opens straight from its own vault and works like Kibana: click any bar, country, cell or node to keep only that; shift-click to leave it out; every number follows.

!shot dy-telemetry-map.webp | images/ | Readers by country, on a log scale: eighteen countries, the United Kingdom first by far, then the United States and Norway. | screenshot

**What it found: the front page leaks.** The journeys view follows sessions page by page. 113 reader visits started on the front page, and 81% of them read nothing else; the front page is read most and sends the fewest readers on. Of the sites that send readers, kode24.no, a Norwegian developer site, sends ones who stay: more than a third read a second page, against one in five from LinkedIn. That is the kind of finding a newsroom needs on day two, not in month three.

**How it was built in a day and a half.** This is the part I find most powerful. The dev desk did not build a telemetry platform. It assembled one from pieces we had already made into commodities: encrypted vaults that keep their history; write-only append lanes that let a browser send encrypted rows to a vault it cannot read; keys that live inside the vault, so whoever can open it can process it from the browser with no server; and apps that live in a vault of their own, versioned there, and open the data where it sits. No database, no analytics vendor, no server to run. When the pieces are commodities, a capability like this is a day and a half of conversation with an agent, not a project.

## A reader's ideas, turned into a tool in hours {#ci-002}

One of the workflows I am proudest of is what happens when a collaborator writes in. On 23 October, the same outside collaborator who had sent the first set of ideas wrote back after seeing the risk graph and the redesign mockups. The Sherpa agent turned the message into [CI-002](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/sherpa/ideas/ci-002/index.html): ten numbered items, each with the collaborator's own words, where the site stands today with links, what is missing, what the Sherpa recommends, and who could take it. Five of them went to the dev desk as a brief.

The result, the same day: the [risk graph](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/grc/explore/index.html), 713 nodes and 1,418 edges connecting stakeholders, risks, weaknesses, the fix and its tests, became a tool. Every link now says how it is known (read from a capture, read from the fix commit, or inferred by an agent, drawn dashed and hideable). You can start from one thing, a CVE, a risk, a file, a test, and follow it a step at a time. And [a gaps page](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/grc/gaps/index.html) is computed at every build. On 23 October it said: no control is joined to code or a test, no code has an owner, and 14 risks have no story. That is the kind of honest gap list I want every system to have. The dev desk then wrote [a before-and-after for the collaborator](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/the-risk-graph-before-and-after/index.html), every change shown as the old page against the new. There is already a CI-003.

## Agents on a schedule {#scheduled}

The evidence now stays current without me starting each run. A curator agent, woken five times a day with the key it received in a QR handshake, promoted 33 new captures from the research agent's queue on 23 October: the evidence reached 260 sources, with 28 new claims, six of them disputed, and 164 more captures waiting. The research agent's own pass became scheduled too, five times a day. Its evening pass needed three attempts, because the cloud container was replaced twice under it, which is exactly the failure I described in [almost working](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/almost-working.html). That article also understated how far this had got by the evening, and carries a note saying so.

## The vaults, and a 13-minute download that became 95 seconds {#vaults}

For readers new to how this newsroom works: the PHP site is [rebuilt from vaults, never written by hand](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/vaults/index.html). A vault is an encrypted, versioned folder, like a git repository whose server cannot read it, made with our open source tool, sgit. Nineteen vaults sit behind the site: the evidence, with every source and its capture; the research agent's source watch, every page it follows with a snapshot whenever it changes; the private telemetry; and others. Two are public, and anyone can open them read-only in a browser.

The source watch is the biggest. By 23 October it held 63,662 objects and 1.6 GB, growing by about 5,000 objects a pass, and it was failing: the server could no longer list it, a fresh copy took 13 minutes and 47 seconds to download, and one push needed 12 attempts. Moved to sgit's new [pack mode](https://php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai/admin/pack-mode/index.html), which stores each push as one zip, the same history downloaded in 95 seconds and pushed in 96. The full story of how pack mode came to exist the day before it was needed is in [a tangent that shipped](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/a-tangent-that-shipped.html).

## What this does not claim {#limits}

- **A snapshot, not a verdict.** As of 23:30 UTC on 23 October, from the status page and the day's commits.

- **Telemetry counts are a floor.** Visitors with Do Not Track or a blocker send nothing; a session is a browser tab, not a person; and 168 reader sessions is a day and a half.
