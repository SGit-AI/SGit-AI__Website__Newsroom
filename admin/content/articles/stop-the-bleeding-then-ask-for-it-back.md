## In short {#in-short}

- **Most of us pay for things we keep and do not use.** Not because we forgot, but because there is data in there, or we might need it, and restarting is painful. The UK government puts the cost of unwanted subscriptions at £1.6 billion a year.
- **Start with a question, not a demand.** Ask the company for a detailed account of our usage, and in parallel do our own analysis. Two sets of numbers are better than one, and the first letter concedes nothing.
- **Then ask for a pause and a refund, nicely.** Say what we used and when, what it was worth, and what is good about the product. We want to keep it, in a suspend mode that keeps the data, and we want the unused months back.
- **Stop the bleeding either way.** Export the data, then pause or cancel. The payments stop whatever the answer to the refund.
- **The law is a floor, not the product.** A refund for months you did not use is generally not owed. The right of access is real, and it is for getting your data, never for leverage. The UK's subscription rules come into force in January 2027.
- **Everything goes into an open register.** Contacts, templates, which companies offer a pause, which refund and which do not, under Creative Commons, recorded as facts with dates rather than adjectives.
- **A service for helpers, run by agents.** 10% of the next three months of savings and 20% of what is recovered. At those rates it only pays if the agent does most of the work, under a behaviour policy where the agent reads and drafts and the person signs in, approves and sends.

## The subscriptions we keep {#keep}

I have a lot of these. You subscribe to something, you use it for a while, there is a bit of data in it, and then you do not really use it. But you might need the capability. And you do not want to stop the whole thing, because on the day you need it again you will not have the time, and restarting is painful: the account, the settings, the history, the integrations. So it sits there, and you keep paying. It is a conferencing tool, a design tool, a storage plan, a research service, a premium tier of something you once needed for a month.

The numbers say this is not just me. In its [consultation on the new subscription regime](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/consultation-on-the-implementation-of-the-new-subscription-contracts-regime) the UK government estimates that 8% of active subscriptions are unwanted, about 9.7 million contracts, and on [9 August 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/pm-starts-roll-out-of-everyday-fixes-on-the-cost-of-living-ending-rip-off-discounts-and-subscription-traps) it put the total at "around 155 million active subscriptions in the UK, with consumers spending an estimated £1.6 billion a year on ones they don't actually want". Citizens Advice [found in 2024](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/about-us/media-centre/press-releases/consumers-spend-688-million-on-unused-subscriptions-in-the-last-year/) that people had spent £688 million in the previous year on subscriptions they did not use.

On [subscriptions.sgit.ai](https://subscriptions.sgit.ai/) I put the principle in one sentence: "A subscription is a discount for committing to regular use. It is not rent on something you have the right to ignore." That site is about the standard and the register. This article is about the other half: a practical service that helps one person, today, with the subscriptions on their statement.

## Step one: ask them what we used, and work it out ourselves {#ask}

The first move is not a legal one. It is a polite email to the company: can you give me a detailed analysis of how much this account has been used? Logins, meetings, storage, seats, documents, by month, for the period I have been paying. It is a reasonable question from a customer, it costs the company little to answer, and it concedes nothing on either side. It is also the question subscriptions.sgit.ai is built on, put slightly differently there: "do you hold a record of my usage?", which every company can answer, even when the answer is no.

In parallel, we do our own analysis, because two sets of numbers are better than one and because we should not depend on the company to know what we did. The statements and invoices say what was paid and when. The account's own dashboards, settings and history pages usually say a lot about what was used: the last meeting, the last upload, the last login, the seats nobody signed into. All of that should be exported now, while we still have access, into a vault that belongs to the person. [The workflows page](https://subscriptions.sgit.ai/workflows/index.html) on the subscriptions site describes the pipeline this reuses, which I ran on my own statements in March: the statements go into a vault, an agent works inside it and commits what it finds, and the output, instead of an accountant's pack, is a file per subscription.

Then the two meet. Where the company's numbers and ours agree, the case is simple. Where they disagree, that is the interesting part, and the next question to ask.

## Step two: a friendly request for a pause and a refund {#request}

A couple of days later, with the evidence in hand, we write back. This letter matters, and its tone is the point. It maps exactly what happened: we paid from this month to this month, we last used it on this date, here is our analysis and here is yours. It says what the product was worth to us when we used it. And it says good things about the product, honestly, because there is a reason we chose it, and there is a reason we did not want to cancel it.

Then it asks for two things. The first is a suspend mode: we like the product and want to keep using it, but not right now, so please put the account into a state that keeps our data and our settings and does not charge us. The second is a refund for the months we did not use.

The first ask is not as unusual as it sounds. Several large services already have a pause button. [Netflix](https://help.netflix.com/en/node/407) pauses a membership "for 1 month", up to three months in total. [Zoom](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0087323) lets small monthly Pro accounts pause for up to three months at a time, which is exactly the conferencing case. YouTube Premium, Audible and Hulu have one too, with conditions. Many do not, and the letter is how a company learns that its customers want one.

## Stop the bleeding {#stop}

Whatever the answer to the refund, the payments should stop. If there is a pause, use it. If there is not, export the data and cancel. This is the part that delivers value on day one, whether or not a single penny comes back, and it is the part that most people never get round to, because each subscription is a small decision that is easy to postpone.

The order matters: export first, then cancel. The data is the reason the account was kept, so losing it in the cancellation would turn a saving into a loss. And watch the terms: some plans charge for leaving early. Adobe's US [subscription terms](https://helpx.adobe.com/manage-account/using/creative-cloud-subscription-terms.html), for example, charge a fee of half the remaining balance for cancelling an annual plan paid monthly after the first fourteen days. Knowing that before the letter is part of the analysis.

## What the law does, and what it does not {#law}

I want to be honest here, because the subscriptions site says it plainly and it is true: "'You did not use it' yields nothing on its own." In general, a company does not have to refund months you paid for and did not use. The money comes back as a legal right only in specific cases: inside a cooling-off period, where the trader did not give you the cancellation information it should have (under the [Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134) that can extend the cancellation period by up to twelve months), for payments taken after you cancelled, or where the service was not provided with reasonable care and skill. Most of the refunds this service asks for will be goodwill.

That is why the request is friendly, and why I think it can work anyway. The direction of travel is clear, and companies will find it harder and harder to justify charging for a period nobody could use. In the UK, the subscription contracts regime in the [Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/part/4/chapter/2) brings reminder notices, an easy exit and a new fourteen-day cooling-off period after a trial or a renewal, and on 9 August 2026 the government said those rules "will now come into force in January 2027", earlier than the spring 2027 date it had given in April. In the EU, a [withdrawal button](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-consumer-protection-law-update-new-mandatory-withdrawal-button-what-online-traders-need-to-know) for online contracts applies from 19 June 2026. In California, [AB 2863](https://dwt.com/insights/2024/10/ab-2863-updates-california-automatic-renewal-law) has required click-to-cancel and annual reminders since July 2025. And in the United States more widely, the FTC's click-to-cancel rule was [vacated by the Eighth Circuit](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/25/07/243137P.pdf) in July 2025, and the FTC [started again](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-seeks-public-comment-response-advance-notice-proposed-rulemaking-regarding-negative-option) in March 2026. None of these rules says "refund the months I ignored". All of them move the default towards the customer.

## If nobody answers: the right of access {#access}

I would not start with a subject access request, and the reason is the tone: the first letter should be the one a good customer sends. But if the first letter gets no answer, the right of access is the next step, and it is a real one. Under UK GDPR, the [ICO's guide](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/subject-access-requests/a-guide-to-subject-access/) says a company "must respond without undue delay, and within one month of receipt of the request", extendable by up to two further months for complex requests, and in most cases cannot charge a fee. The European Data Protection Board's [guidelines on the right of access](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-012022-data-subject-rights-right-access_en) list "activity logs such as access logs, history of website usage" among the data a person can ask for, so the usage record of a personal account is in scope. The ICO also confirms that a third party with the appropriate authority can make the request on someone's behalf, which is the helper's position.

One rule, from the subscriptions site, matters more than the mechanics: "Every access request is a genuine request for the person's own data, sent because they want it. It is never sent as leverage, never bundled with a demand." An access request used as a threat is how the mechanism gets refused and how it loses its value for everybody else. So the access request asks for the data, and the refund conversation stays a separate conversation.

## An open register, under Creative Commons {#register}

There are a couple of services in this, but I think we should open source the whole method, under Creative Commons, and publish what we learn as we figure it out. For every company: how to reach the people who can actually decide, the templates that worked, whether a pause exists, how long they took to answer, and what they said.

That includes the companies that refund and the companies that do not. I want to give real kudos to the companies that do it, because they are not obliged to, and that deserves to be said loudly. And I want to have the data on the companies that say no. The subscriptions site has already worked out how to publish that safely, and I would follow its rules exactly: record, never verdict, with no adjectives anywhere; the reply quoted with the person's details removed; silence recorded as a row, not a blank; "we do not hold that" recorded neutrally; never publish a first refusal, only one given after a second ask; and the good path published as prominently as the bad. A dated row that says "asked on this date, answered on that date, refunded three months" is the kudos. A dated row that says "asked twice, no reply as at this date" needs no adjective. The register publishes a company's behaviour, never a person's case.

## A service for helpers {#helper}

This is a service I think a lot of people could provide, and that a lot of people would use: a freelancer, a bookkeeper, a virtual assistant, a student, anybody organised and persistent, working for an individual or a small business. The helper is paid from what they save. My suggestion: **10% of the savings for the next three months, and 20% of any money recovered.** Nothing saved, nothing paid.

That is deliberately low. For comparison, Rocket Money's bill negotiation [charges](https://help.rocketmoney.com/en/articles/9744474-bill-negotiation-charge-explained) "a fee of 35% - 60% of your first year's savings". And it is worth doing the arithmetic, because it explains the rest of this design. The government's figure for the average unwanted subscription is £14 a month. Stopping one earns the helper 10% of three months of that, £4.20. Recovering six months of it, £84, earns 20%, £16.80. Per subscription those are small numbers. They add up only across many subscriptions per client and many clients per helper, which means the helper cannot be doing the analysis, the letters and the follow-ups by hand.

## Done by agents, under a policy {#agents}

All of this should be done agentically, through the setup I already run: an account of the agent's own connected to Claude, a mailbox of its own, a vault per piece of work, and a behaviour policy written before the first run, as described in [Replicating the agentic inbox](/articles/replicating-the-agentic-inbox.html). The agent does what agents are good at: reading statements, building the inventory, comparing two sets of numbers, drafting the letters, chasing the replies, keeping the record and preparing the register row. The helper reviews. The person whose money it is approves and sends, from their own address, because the company deals with the account holder, and because, as I argued in [Who owns the words](/articles/who-owns-the-words.html), the agent can make decisions but the responsibility stays with the person who signs. Every draft passes [a second reader the agent cannot skip](/articles/a-second-reader-the-agent-cannot-skip.html) before anyone sees it.

The policy is where the hard part becomes visible. This work needs access to a lot of systems: every subscription is an account, with its own login, and the usage history and the export button are behind it. A signed-in session can do far more than read. The same login that downloads the invoices can delete the data, close the account, upgrade the plan or change the card. So the policy has to say, row by row, what the agent could do, what it is meant to do, and what stands in the way, and today, honestly, several of those rows are held only by an expectation.

And one row is not solved: the agent must never hold the person's passwords. The direction is right in the products that keep credentials apart from the agent, as I wrote about Meta's design in [A personal agent that keeps your secrets](/articles/a-personal-agent-that-keeps-your-secrets.html), where the agent "has no visibility into people's passwords or payment methods". For this service I think the answer is that the person signs in and the agent works inside that session, with the session's reach written into the policy, but how to do that well, across fifty different login pages, is the thing we need to figure out, and I would rather say so than pretend.

## What this does not claim {#limits}

- **It has not been run as a service.** The workflow, the fees and the register rules are proposals. The statement pipeline it reuses ran in March on my own statements; nothing else here has been tested with a client.
- **Most refunds will be goodwill.** Unused months are generally not refundable as of right; the legal levers are narrower, and this is not legal advice.
- **The consumer rules may not cover business accounts.** The UK and EU rules quoted protect consumers. A sole trader or small company paying for a business tool may not be one, and the request then rests on goodwill alone.
- **The law is as at 13 October 2026.** The UK regime is not in force until January 2027; the EU button depends on national transposition; the US federal rule is being written again.
- **The fee split is a suggestion.** Contingency fees can change how a service looks to a regulator, and the card-issuer route is left out until there is advice on it, as the subscriptions site says.
- **The register has no entries yet.** Its rules are written; a solicitor review of the publishing rule is still listed as pending on subscriptions.sgit.ai.
- **The login problem is open.** The behaviour policy in the figure names it; it does not solve it.
