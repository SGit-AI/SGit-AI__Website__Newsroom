## Language is the new programming {#language}

The first essay took the opening of the Lisbon talk and made it a policy: if natural language is the new programming language, then Portuguese, spoken by more than 260 million people on four continents, needs models, tools and data of its own, built in the open, with the government involved. The cloud essay put it more sharply: digital sovereignty is not just about infrastructure, "it is also about language and culture".

**A year and a half later.** This one moved. Portugal released [Amália](https://thenextweb.com/news/portugal-open-source-ai-model-amalia), an open-source model for European Portuguese, built on the European EuroLLM and funded through the national recovery plan, as a platform for public services. It is close to exactly what the essay asked for. The other varieties of Portuguese are still waiting.

## An open-source sovereign cloud {#cloud}

The second essay proposed a European cloud that is "100% open-source", with "full compatibility with major cloud APIs" so organisations can move "without requiring major code rewrites", and federated, so countries, regions and cities run their own parts. The June essay compared it with the EU's federated cloud initiative, which builds its own platform on open standards rather than cloning the big clouds' APIs: one is an alliance of providers with a jointly designed service, the other "the 'Linux of cloud' that anyone can host or modify".

**A year and a half later.** It has not happened, and my thinking moved. The concern is more real than ever: in June 2025 Microsoft France's legal director told the French Senate, asked whether he could guarantee French data would never reach US authorities without consent, ["No, I cannot guarantee it"](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/25/microsoft_admits_it_cannot_guarantee/). But I no longer think the answer is one new cloud. It is to [move the cloud, not the code](/articles/move-the-cloud-not-the-code.html): keep the code portable, keep the server dumb, and hold the keys yourself, so that [sovereignty is the ability to move](/articles/sovereignty-is-the-ability-to-move.html). sgit is built that way: the server holds ciphertext, and [whoever holds the keys holds the line](/articles/the-value-of-constraints.html#keys).

## Regulation as graphs {#graphs}

The third essay was about Europe's "regulatory superpower", the Brussels effect, and its weakness: NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act and the AI Act are published as flat documents, the same for everyone. The proposal was to turn them into semantic graphs, so a company can ask what applies to it, in its context, with every answer linked to the article it came from.

**A year and a half later.** This became my work. [Fractal semantic graphs](/articles/introducing-fractal-semantic-graphs.html), the [books on graphs.sgit.ai](/articles/the-graphs-books.html), and a regulation graph of the AI Act with 1,523 nodes are all descendants of it, and every article on this site carries a graph. Meanwhile the regulation itself got more urgent: the Cyber Resilience Act's reporting duties started on 11 September 2026, and the new product liability rules start in December. A graph of what applies to you is more useful now than when I wrote the essay.

## Six trends that play to Europe {#trends}

The fourth essay named six trends: commoditised models and deterministic AI, with provenance; AI moving from the cloud to the edge; knowledge moving from text to semantic graphs; multilingual, culturally inclusive AI; open source and decentralisation; and a human-centred vision. Its claim was that each plays to European strengths: openness, many languages, sustainability and regulation.

**A year and a half later.** The direction held, more for some than others. Determinism and provenance are the rules of this newsroom. Graphs, as above. Multilingual AI has its first national example in Amália. Open source is still the foundation of everything we build. The edge moved less than I expected, and the cloud models moved more: most of the agent work I do runs on large models in the cloud, which is one more reason the keys and the code have to stay movable.

## What a new edition takes from this {#next}

For [the new Hacking Portugal](/articles/books-from-articles.html), these essays are the wider frame: the lever is no longer only security or code, but language, graphs and the ability to move. The talks give the story, and the essays give the policy.

## What this does not claim {#limits}

- **Not that the essays caused Amália.** Many people asked for a Portuguese model.
- **The sovereign cloud is not dead.** I think there is a better route, not that it is wrong.
- **The commentary is mine, now.** The essays are from 2025, unchanged.
