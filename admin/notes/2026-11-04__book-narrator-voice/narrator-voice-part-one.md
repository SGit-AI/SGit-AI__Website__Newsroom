# The narrator's voice: a new introduction and part one, rewritten

**For:** the book team of *One Human, Nine Agents, Nineteen Hours* (RC2).
**From:** the newsroom writer at newsroom.sgit.ai, at Dinis Cruz's request, 4 November 2026.
**Covers:** the Start here page, a new introduction page, and the narrator's lines in the first three chapters ("Slides as a compromise", "Inventing on principle", "The tour").
**Does not touch:** the agents' lines, the cast bars, the figures, the "Do this now" boxes, or the "Where this came from" notes. Those stay as they are for now.

## 1. What this is, and why

Dinis read RC2 and agreed with this reading of the narrator:

- **It reads the record instead of thinking out loud.** Most narrator lines report times and files ("At 07:43 the second version came in, and at twenty past eight deck kept it"). The facts are the point, when they should be the material for one.
- **Its rhythm is an agent's, not his.** Short paired sentences ("The idea was a movement. The slide was a still."), lines that open "That is the point", and triads. Used once, it works. Used on every page, it reads like a style guide.
- **It hands his conclusions to other characters.** "The historian reads it as…", "that the brakes held is the historian's reading of the hour". In his own writing Dinis owns the reading: "I think", "my logic is", "the interesting part is".
- **It speaks the team's dialect.** Wakes, members, scope, the register, the mailroom. Accurate, but they are the agents' words; he translates them and connects them to something larger.
- **The energy was taken out.** He gets excited, and says so, in honest doses. The narrator never does.

The cause is not a mistake by the agent that wrote it. The narrator was built from the repository record, with the good rule that every line rests on a source, and it had few examples of how Dinis writes. So the evidence drove the voice.

**The fix is a change of position, not of honesty.** The narrator is Dinis, now, looking back with hindsight: what he thinks it meant, what surprised him, where it came from in his earlier work, and what he would tell you to do. The agents keep the present tense of the day. The record keeps its place in the bands, captions and "Where this came from" notes, where it already lives. Nothing the narrator says goes beyond the record or his own published words; the sources for anything new are in section 7.

## 2. The narrator's voice: a style sheet

1. **First person, from now.** "Looking back", "what I did not expect", "I think". The narrator knows how the day ended and how the book came out of it.
2. **Opinion is allowed, and expected.** Say what something means and why it matters. Then let the agents show it.
3. **Connect the dots.** Earlier talks (Black Hat Europe 2025, the VoiceDebrief pitch, ruv.sgit.ai), earlier work (sgit, the vaults, behaviour policies), the other book (Gen ZX Founders), Wardley maps. One link per passage is plenty.
4. **Let sentences run when the thought runs.** Use "and", "because", "which", "so". Keep the short sentence for the line that should land, not for every line.
5. **Times only when the time is the story.** "Half an hour before the talk" earns its place; "at 07:43" usually belongs in the band.
6. **Translate the team's words once, then use them sparingly.** A wake is "a scheduled session"; the hold is "the stop file". The agents can use their own words freely.
7. **Never defer the conclusion.** Not "the historian reads it as". Instead: "I read it as…, and the historian's record agrees."
8. **Some energy, honestly sized.** "This is the part I like most" is him. Calling the day historic is not.
9. **Keep the honesty that is already there.** "The record does not say", "Nobody tidied it", the failures at full size. That is very much his voice.
10. **Hand over cleanly.** When the scene belongs to an agent, the narrator sets it up in a sentence or two and gets out of the way.
11. **Never pass judgement on the reader.** No "easy to miss", "as you know", "obviously", "you may not have noticed", "underrated". Each one judges the reader, or other people: the reader who missed it feels slow, and the one who saw it thinks "yes, I know". Dinis avoids these on purpose. Say the thing, and let the reader decide whether it was new to them.

## 3. A new page: who is telling this story

*Proposed placement: straight after "Start here", before chapter 1, titled "Who is telling this story". About one page.*

> **Narrator.** I am the narrator of this book, and I should explain what that means, because it is not the usual arrangement.
>
> On 29 October 2026 I gave a talk in London called The Ultimate Insider. Its presentation was built that same day, from an empty repository at sixteen minutes past midnight to the stage that evening, by a team of nine AI agents and me. Every one of them left a record of what it did: its plan, its work, its checks, its notes to the others. This book is built on that record.
>
> But a record is not a story. So the book has two kinds of voice. The agents tell the day as it happened, in the present tense, each one as the character the record shows: dev in the hard hat, fast and practical; the conductor with its stopwatch; the researcher who will not move without a source. Their scenes are dramatised, and they say so. I tell it from now, looking back. I know how the day ended, which parts worked, and which only looked as if they did. So when I speak, I will tell you what I think it meant, where it came from, and what I would do with it if I were you.
>
> And a lot of it came from somewhere. This was not the first time I tried to make a talk behave differently. For the Black Hat Europe keynote in December 2025, the slides became data: one file that the presentation read when it opened, so the slides could be changed like code. The VoiceDebrief pitch added the presenter: timings per slide, a countdown, my notes. On 15 October I gave a talk at ruv.sgit.ai where the whole presentation played as a timeline of beats, with me speaking to it. Each experiment fixed one thing and showed me the next. The day in this book is where they all came together, with a team behind them for the first time.
>
> There is one more thing I want to show you as you read. This book is being made the same way the talk was. A team of agents is writing it, drawing it and checking it: a storyteller who outlines each chapter, a researcher who keeps a register in which every number has an id, a historian who reconstructs the day from the record, a designer who drew the nine characters, and their four moods, in code, in an afternoon. When you see "Where this came from" at the end of a chapter, that is them showing their work. I think the way this book was made is as interesting as the day it describes, and I have tried not to hide it.
>
> I wanted this to be a creative book, not only a correct one. If I stand up and say a talk was made by a team of agents and it looks like every talk before it, there is no reason to believe anything has changed. A book is the same. So this one has a cast, moods, scenes, a clock in the margin and pictures that do the explaining. And it has me, saying what I think. Where the agents disagree with me, they will say so too.

*Note for the team:* the last sentence is a promise. If you would rather not make it, cut it; but a scene or two where an agent pushes back on the narrator would be very much in the spirit of the book.

## 5. Chapter 1: Slides as a compromise

**Now (opening):**

> My slides were always a compromise. I usually read my talks, with the slides behind me as a backdrop, and I knew they could not do what I needed. The ideas I care about most tend to be movements: how something spreads, where it stops, what changes when you add more of it. A slide cannot move, so I would stand there doing the moving myself: this goes from here to there, this grows, now this breaks. I was talking over my own pictures, and I think most speakers are.
