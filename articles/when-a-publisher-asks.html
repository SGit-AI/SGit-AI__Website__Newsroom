When a publisher asks: exploring offers to license a book that started on Leanpub, which technical publishers take books like this, what they publish about their terms, the books that went from Leanpub or a free online edition to a publisher before, the questions to ask before signing anything, and what I want to keep for One Human, Nine Agents, Nineteen Hours

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-11-19 · article v1.0.0 · newsroom v0.14.178 · bookspublishingleanpublicensingrightsstrategyarticle

Abstract: A technical publisher has asked whether my book, One Human, Nine Agents, Nineteen Hours, is available for licensing. It is the first such approach, and probably not the last, so this is how I want to think about all of them, not one. Several technical publishers take books that began elsewhere, even if few say so on their author pages: Clojure for the Brave and True and Understanding ECMAScript 6 went from Leanpub to No Starch, Writing APIs With Lumen from Leanpub to Apress, Think Python from a self-published free book to O'Reilly, which kept its Creative Commons licence. What publishers publish about terms varies: the Pragmatic Bookshelf says royalties start at 42%, No Starch shows a menu from 15% with no advance to 10% with an $8,000 one and an exclusive grant in all forms and languages for the life of copyright, others say only "competitive". Leanpub's own terms are the starting point: the author keeps the copyright, Leanpub's licence is non-exclusive, and Leanpub suggests asking a publisher to carve out the ebook. So my questions are about scope: which formats, languages and territories, for how long, whether the Leanpub edition stays on sale with its updates, and what comes back if the publisher does not use it.

In short

  • A publisher has asked to license the book. The first such approach; probably not the last.
  • Several technical publishers take books that began elsewhere, and there is a record of it.
  • Leanpub's terms leave the rights with the author, and its licence is non-exclusive.
  • The questions are about scope: formats, languages, territories, term, and whether the Leanpub edition stays.
  • What I want to keep: the open, updated Leanpub edition.

The approach

A technical publisher wrote to say it had found One Human, Nine Agents, Nineteen Hours on Leanpub, that its practical approach to agent orchestration fits what it wants to bring to its readers, and to ask whether the book is available for licensing. The book has been on Leanpub since 14 November, free as a minimum, 19 dollars suggested, with every update included (the book is on Leanpub), and a print edition is close (where to sell the book and the appliances).

I am glad of it, and I want to answer it well. But it is better to think about the whole landscape first, because the same question will come from others, and the answer should be one I would give to any of them.

Who takes books like this

Few technical publishers say on their author pages that they will take a book already on sale elsewhere, but several have done it, and their pages say different amounts about terms:

  • The Pragmatic Bookshelf publishes royalties "starting at 42% for first-time authors", across books, translations and licensed content, and also offers self-published authors its shop and marketing (publish with us).
  • No Starch Press shows its menu openly: 15% with no advance, 12% with $5,000, or 10% with $8,000; 25% on ebooks; translation income split equally (write for us). Its standard contract grants exclusive rights in print, electronic "or any other form", in all languages, worldwide, for the life of copyright, with unused subsidiary rights reverting on request after two years (its agreement).
  • Manning says it discusses terms "once we have a solid plan" (write for us); Packt promises "competitive advances and royalties" (contributors); O'Reilly invites proposals (work with us). Apress, part of Springer Nature, and Wiley have author pages that we could not read on the day.
  • CRC Press and Taylor & Francis note that previously published material needs permission (proposals).

It has been done before

  • Clojure for the Brave and True, by Daniel Higginbotham, went from Leanpub to No Starch in 2015; its Leanpub page said it "will be published by No Starch Press when it's complete" (Leanpub).
  • Understanding ECMAScript 6, by Nicholas C. Zakas, went from Leanpub to No Starch in 2016, with the open text kept under a Creative Commons licence (its repository).
  • Writing APIs With Lumen, by Paul Redmond, went from Leanpub to Apress as Lumen Programming Guide in 2016 (Leanpub podcast).
  • Think Python, by Allen Downey, was a free self-published book before O'Reilly published it in 2012, keeping its Creative Commons licence (O'Reilly's copyright page).
  • Eloquent JavaScript, by Marijn Haverbeke, is free online under Creative Commons and also published on paper by No Starch (the book).

The pattern is encouraging: an open or early edition did not stop a publisher; in several cases it is why the publisher came.

Where Leanpub stands

Leanpub's terms are clear: "Leanpub Authors or Organizations retain the copyright to their work"; the licence to Leanpub is non-exclusive, and authors are free to sell a book on Leanpub and elsewhere at the same time (Leanpub's terms). If a publisher is interested, Leanpub suggests asking it to carve out the ebook rights, and advises researching any offer very carefully (Leanpub's help). It is also candid that a traditional deal usually means lower royalties, less independence and some rights signed away (approaching a publisher).

The questions to ask

Drawn from the Authors Guild, the Alliance of Independent Authors and Leanpub's own advice:

  • License, do not assign, and limit the grant to the formats, languages and territories the publisher will actually use; anything not named stays with the author (Authors Guild, ALLi).
  • Can the Leanpub edition stay on sale, with its updates and its pay-what-you-want price?
  • For how long, and what brings the rights back: reversion tied to sales, not to the book being "available", since print on demand keeps every book available for ever (Authors Guild on reversion).
  • The royalty and the advance, on what base, and the ebook rate; the Authors Guild argues for half of net on ebooks (Authors Guild on ebooks).
  • Translations and audio: keep them, or give them a use-it-or-lose-it term.
  • A non-compete clause, and how wide it is, since the next books will be on the same subjects.
  • The title, the edits and the price: who decides.
  • An open licence, if the book has one: publishers have kept Creative Commons licences before, but the rest of the contract still matters (Authors Alliance).

Other routes for the same book

  • Print of my own, through KDP or IngramSpark, while the ebook stays on Leanpub, which Leanpub supports (print on Amazon, ebook on Leanpub).
  • Translation rights, licensed language by language, which Leanpub leaves entirely to the author (translation rights); a foreign-rights agent usually takes 20% and nothing up front (ALLi on foreign rights).
  • Audio, for example through ACX, at different rates for exclusive and non-exclusive distribution (ACX royalties).

What I want to keep

For this book, the answer I would like to reach with any publisher is a licence, not a sale: the publisher brings editing, print, distribution and marketing in the formats and places it will really use, and the Leanpub edition stays open, updated and pay-what-you-want, because that is how the book was made and how readers have found it. If that is possible, a publisher widens the audience without closing the door the book came through. That is the conversation I will start.

What this does not claim

  • No terms have been offered or discussed; this is preparation.
  • Royalty figures are as each publisher's own page showed on 19 November 2026; most publishers do not publish them, and some pages could not be read.
  • This is not legal advice; any contract will be read with someone who reads them for a living.
