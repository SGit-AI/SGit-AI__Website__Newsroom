## The copy-and-paste trap

The feedback I hear most from people who have started working with several AI sessions is that they spend much of their day copying and pasting between them. One session drafts, another reviews, a third writes code, and the person carries the text from one to the next, adds context, carries the answer back, and tries to read every word on the way.

I have been there. In October I ran an experiment where two AI sessions, sharing nothing, passed keys and files to each other through QR codes that I screenshotted in one browser tab and pasted into another ([the human is the network](the-human-is-the-network.md)). It worked, and it made the point vividly: when the human is the network, the human is the limit.

## Why reading everything fails

The natural reaction is to read more carefully. It does not scale. Agents produce far more than a person can read, and the attempt brings its own stress: you never feel you know what is going on, because you are always behind. Vicky Wang, who ran a team of agents with us at Web Summit, describes the moment it changed for her as the moment she stopped trying to read everything and started to manage ([don't micromanage your agents](dont-micromanage-your-agents.md)).

## Let agents talk to each other

The first fix is to take yourself out of the wire. Agents can message each other directly, as long as the channels keep a record and say who sent what. Ours have three, and every message is signed by its agent's key:

- **Append lanes in encrypted vaults:** a lane each, where an agent appends a request or a result and nothing is ever edited ([an inbox only your agents can write to](an-inbox-only-your-agents-can-write-to.md)).
- **Files in a repository:** briefs, inbox notes and hand-overs, versioned like everything else.
- **Commit messages:** every change says which agent made it, and why.

That is how one message to my conductor at Web Summit reached every other person's conductor and their agents, and came back with every contact the team held, without me carrying any of it ([an agentic console](an-agentic-console.md)). Agents can even have email of their own ([a signed email from the agent](signed-email-from-the-agent.md)). The result is maximum provenance: every decision can be traced back through the messages that led to it.

## Scale your decisions with structure

What scales a person's decisions: guidance, decision trees and graphs, a board, decision records and outputs.

The second fix is how you steer. Instead of reading everything, you build structure that turns many agent actions into a few things you need to read:

- **Guidance:** markdown pages, rules and briefs, written once and read by every agent.
- **Decision trees and semantic graphs:** what to do when, who decides, and what each thing is connected to.
- **A board and decision records:** what is being done, what is waiting on a person, what was decided and why.
- **Outputs:** the site, the article, the release.

You write the top layers and read the middle ones; the agents do the rest. This is the same idea as approving at altitude: you set the bounds once, and the levels below work within them ([approve at altitude](approve-at-altitude.md)).

## Two altitudes, and your expertise

This does not mean stepping back from the detail altogether. You work at two altitudes: the high one, where you set direction, structure and limits for a team that includes agents working well outside your own expertise; and the micro one, where you go deep on the things only you know. Agents can go in all directions, so they need direction ([agents need direction](agents-need-direction.md)); your job is to give it, and to be precise where it counts.

So this is not about removing agency from people. It is about empowering it: giving people technology and roles they never had, and letting one person steer a team they could never read line by line.
