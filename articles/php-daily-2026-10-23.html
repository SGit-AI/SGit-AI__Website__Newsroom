PHP October 2026, update 2: the story held, a site that watches its own readers, agents on a schedule, and the decisions that wait on me (23 October, 23:30 UTC)

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-10-24 · article v1.2.0, 3 versions · newsroom v0.13.66 · php2shellupdatesagentsobservabilityevidencearticle

Abstract: The second update on php2shell and the newsroom covering it, as of 23:30 UTC on 23 October. The story held: no named organisation has said it was breached through php2shell, CISA still lists neither flaw as exploited, and 109 of 751 affected products in the Dutch NCSC's inventory are fixed. The newsroom changed more. In a day and a half it built tools to see who reads the site and what works on it, private by design, and they found that the front page leaks: 81% of readers who start there read nothing else. An outside collaborator's five ideas turned the risk graph into a tool within hours. Scheduled agents now keep the evidence current, and our biggest data vault went from a 13-minute download to 95 seconds. And 15 of the 20 agents were waiting on a decision from me.

Update 2. Following update 1, PHP October 2026, at 8pm on 22 October, here is where things stood at 23:30 UTC on 23 October. Update 3 follows, as of 23:40 UTC on 24 October. The updates so far are listed at the end.

In short

  • The story held. No named organisation breached through php2shell; neither flaw on CISA's exploited list; 109 of 751 products fixed.
  • The site now watches its own readers, privately. And the first thing it showed is that the front page leaks.
  • A reader's ideas became a tool in hours. The risk graph, rebuilt from a collaborator's five ideas.
  • Readers have a guide. A new agent answers their feedback point by point.
  • Agents on a schedule keep the evidence current. 33 new captures on 23 October.
  • The bottleneck is me. 15 of the 20 agents were waiting on my decisions.

The story

The journalist agent keeps the state of affairs as a versioned page, so the day can be read as the difference between two versions. Version 1, at 14:11 UTC:

  • Organisations that have said they were breached through php2shell: none. Three cases still open (ČVUT, which stopped an attack it links to worldwide PHP attempts; GMO's FX VPS, with no cause stated; DMM, with PHP not confirmed) and one ruled out (Valtec, which corrected itself).
  • CISA's catalogue of known exploited vulnerabilities: neither flaw listed, unchanged since the evening of 22 October.
  • NVD's score: 9.8 out of 10 (CVSS 3.1), beside the 9.2 (CVSS 4.0) the PHP project gave it.
  • Public code repositories mentioning the flaws: 735, up from 586 the evening before. A count of search results, not of working exploits.
  • The Dutch NCSC's inventory of affected software: 109 of 751 products fixed, 389 still under investigation.

Version 2 followed just after midnight, from 295 sources, with six of the curator's new claims held back and the reason given. The line that matters most held all day: no named organisation said it was broken into through php2shell. That is a time-bounded absence, and the site says so; one statement from one organisation would end it.

A site that watches its own readers

The biggest change of the day was on the newsroom's side. In a day and a half, the dev desk agent built the tools I had been asking for: a way to see who reads the site and what works on it, without tracking anyone.

Private by design. Every page sends one anonymous event: the page, the site that referred the reader, a country and city from a lookup, the language, the kind of screen and a random id that lives in that browser tab. No IP address, no cookie, no name. The event is encrypted in the reader's browser before it leaves, and sent to a write-only lane on a private vault: the lane accepts writes and answers every one the same way, so nobody can read anything back through it. Only the holder of the vault can read the events.

Not everyone who opens a page is a reader. The first night looked busy: 356 page views from one session. It was Google's crawler, working through 252 pages. So every session is now in one of five groups: readers, the editor, power users and the team, synthetic visitors (agents and tests, including our own), and crawlers. Of the 1,093 page views by 20:26 UTC, crawlers were 565 and readers 233. Without the groups, every chart would have been mostly Google.

A public face and a private dashboard. The counts, and only the counts, go into a public vault anyone can open, with a world map of where readers are. The full events stay private, in a dashboard that opens straight from its own vault and works like Kibana: click any bar, country, cell or node to keep only that; shift-click to leave it out; every number follows.

What it found: the front page leaks. The journeys view follows sessions page by page. 113 reader visits started on the front page, and 81% of them read nothing else; the front page is read most and sends the fewest readers on. Of the sites that send readers, kode24.no, a Norwegian developer site, sends ones who stay: more than a third read a second page, against one in five from LinkedIn. That is the kind of finding a newsroom needs on day two, not in month three.

How it was built in a day and a half. This is the part I find most powerful. The dev desk did not build a telemetry platform. It assembled one from pieces we had already made into commodities: encrypted vaults that keep their history; write-only append lanes that let a browser send encrypted rows to a vault it cannot read; keys that live inside the vault, so whoever can open it can process it from the browser with no server; and apps that live in a vault of their own, versioned there, and open the data where it sits. No database, no analytics vendor, no server to run. When the pieces are commodities, a capability like this is a day and a half of conversation with an agent, not a project.

A reader's ideas, turned into a tool in hours

One of the workflows I am proudest of is what happens when a collaborator writes in. On 23 October, the same outside collaborator who had sent the first set of ideas wrote back after seeing the risk graph and the redesign mockups. The Sherpa agent turned the message into CI-002: ten numbered items, each with the collaborator's own words, where the site stands today with links, what is missing, what the Sherpa recommends, and who could take it. Five of them went to the dev desk as a brief.

The result, the same day: the risk graph, 713 nodes and 1,418 edges connecting stakeholders, risks, weaknesses, the fix and its tests, became a tool. Every link now says how it is known (read from a capture, read from the fix commit, or inferred by an agent, drawn dashed and hideable). You can start from one thing, a CVE, a risk, a file, a test, and follow it a step at a time. And a gaps page is computed at every build. On 23 October it said: no control is joined to code or a test, no code has an owner, and 14 risks have no story. That is the kind of honest gap list I want every system to have. The dev desk then wrote a before-and-after for the collaborator, every change shown as the old page against the new. There is already a CI-003.

Readers have a guide

A new agent joined: the Sherpa, which walks with the reader. It answers readers' feedback point by point (three pieces on 23 October, with two interface bugs reproduced), keeps a friction log of what gets in readers' way and who can fix each, and sets collaborators' ideas against what the site already does. The designer put a redesign out for review: two dials, who you are and how much of the site you see (Focus, Standard, Everything), and four mockups built from the live data. No reader page has changed yet; the picks are mine.

Agents on a schedule

The evidence now stays current without me starting each run. A curator agent, woken five times a day with the key it received in a QR handshake, promoted 33 new captures from the research agent's queue on 23 October: the evidence reached 260 sources, with 28 new claims, six of them disputed, and 164 more captures waiting. The research agent's own pass became scheduled too, five times a day. Its evening pass needed three attempts, because the cloud container was replaced twice under it, which is exactly the failure I described in almost working. That article also understated how far this had got by the evening, and carries a note saying so.

Security, as it went

Two things worth recording. A vault's key is the whole boundary: whoever holds it can read and drain that vault, so the vaults page now lists the keys each agent holds, by ID and kind only, never the keys. And about an hour after this snapshot, a second security pass found and fixed a stored cross-site scripting flaw in the Ask pane, where a captured page's title could have broken out of a citation and run script.

What waits on me

The team's status page is blunt about it: 20 agents, 2 working, 2 blocked, 15 waiting on me, 10 branches waiting to be released, and $588 spent across all the sessions so far. At the top of its list: a go to publish the evidence the curator has added (the site shows 227 sources against 260 in the vault), the redesign picks, two blocked agents whose sessions cannot push, a security review of two branches, and two sealed keys to open before they expire. That list is the real lesson of the day. The agents are fast; the decisions are mine, and that is where the time goes.

The vaults, and a 13-minute download that became 95 seconds

For readers new to how this newsroom works: the PHP site is rebuilt from vaults, never written by hand. A vault is an encrypted, versioned folder, like a git repository whose server cannot read it, made with our open source tool, sgit. Nineteen vaults sit behind the site: the evidence, with every source and its capture; the research agent's source watch, every page it follows with a snapshot whenever it changes; the private telemetry; and others. Two are public, and anyone can open them read-only in a browser.

The source watch is the biggest. By 23 October it held 63,662 objects and 1.6 GB, growing by about 5,000 objects a pass, and it was failing: the server could no longer list it, a fresh copy took 13 minutes and 47 seconds to download, and one push needed 12 attempts. Moved to sgit's new pack mode, which stores each push as one zip, the same history downloaded in 95 seconds and pushed in 96. The full story of how pack mode came to exist the day before it was needed is in a tangent that shipped.

Why these regular updates

A live incident moves faster than any single article. These updates are fixed snapshots rather than a running commentary, so that anyone can go back and see where things stood, and what changed between them. Each is taken at a moment the data is consistent; for this one, 23:30 UTC, when the conductor agent refreshed the team's status page, reading all twenty agent sessions, the repository and the scheduled tasks without asking any agent. A few things that landed in the hour after are marked as such.

The updates so far

  1. PHP October 2026, at 8pm: update 1, the evening of 22 October, with a guided tour of the site.
  2. This article: update 2, 23:30 UTC on 23 October.
  3. PHP October 2026, update 3: 23:40 UTC on 24 October.

What this does not claim

  • A snapshot, not a verdict. As of 23:30 UTC on 23 October, from the status page and the day's commits.
  • The story's numbers are the journalist's version 1. Version 2 came after the snapshot.
  • Telemetry counts are a floor. Visitors with Do Not Track or a blocker send nothing; a session is a browser tab, not a person; and 168 reader sessions is a day and a half.
  • Costs are each session's running total at its latest message, as the status page reports them.
  • No breach named is time-bounded. One statement from one organisation would end it.
