The exit is when you sell control: why a founder should leave the day someone else is calling the shots, and why to negotiate that day at the start, the Mourinho clause

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-10-20 · article v1.2.0, 3 versions · newsroom v0.12.150 · startupsfoundersexitscontrolgovernanceopen-sourcearticle

Abstract: What is a good exit for a founder? Fundamentally, it is somebody buying the company: buying control of what you created. And control can be sold long before the shares are. A founder can own 50 or 60% and not be calling the shots, because the board and the investors' vetoes decide; another can own 14% and hold 61% of the votes. My argument is that the founder's exit is the day control changes hands, and that is the day to leave. Founders want to create. When the strategy is no longer theirs, and the company moves from explorer to villager and town planner, the founder's energy is no longer what it needs, and staying costs them their health, their sanity and what they could build next, for a couple more zeros. The research agrees that most founders lose the top job early: in Noam Wasserman's study, half were no longer CEO by year three, and four in five were forced to step down. So negotiate the exit at the start, while you have leverage. I call it the Mourinho clause: his exits, whatever caused them, were priced in his contracts before anyone needed them. And write down now the number that would make you happy, so that later you recognise when you have passed it.

In short

  • The exit is when somebody buys control. Not necessarily all the shares.
  • Control can go long before ownership. A board majority and investor vetoes are enough.
  • That is the day to leave. When the strategy is no longer yours, move on.
  • Founders want to create. The town planner phase needs someone else's energy.
  • Most founders lose the top job early. Half within three years, in Wasserman's study.
  • Negotiate the exit at the start. The Mourinho clause: the exit priced before anyone needs it.
  • Write your number down now. And notice when you have passed it.

What an exit is

A good exit for a founder is somebody buying the company. That is fundamentally what it is: somebody buys what you created, the technology, the service, the team, the customers. The question that follows is whether you sell all your shares or keep some. But I think the shares are the wrong way to look at it. The exit is when somebody buys control.

Control and ownership are not the same thing, and they part company early. A founder can own 50 or 60% of a company and not be calling the shots. Investors who join in later rounds usually take board seats and protective provisions, the right to veto a sale, new shares, new debt or the budget. As a Cooley partner put the investor's position, "thou shall not do anything that will impact my investment… without my blessing". Add a drag-along, which lets a majority force the others to sell on the same terms, and the founder with the most shares may be the one with the least say.

It works the other way too. Mark Zuckerberg owns about 13.6% of Meta's shares and holds 61.0% of the votes, because his Class B shares carry ten votes each, according to Meta's 2025 proxy statement. He has sold most of the company and kept control of it.

So the exit is not the day you sell your last share. It is the day you are no longer the one deciding.

That is the day to leave

My argument is that the founder should move on as soon as they lose control. As soon as they are not calling the shots, as soon as the strategy is not fully in line with what they want to do, that is the time to go. It could be a moment when they still own 50 or 60% or more.

Founders want to found. They want to create, to be in the creative flow for as long as they can, and to be productive. In the terms this site uses, a founder is an explorer. Companies move on from that: they become villagers, with process and a board, and then town planners, run for reliability and for value. That is sometimes exactly what the investors want, and an acquirer who buys a product because they like it and want to extract as much value as they can from it is making a pure town planner play. That can be exactly what the company needs. It is a different game from the founder's.

What worries me is how many brilliant people I know, more intelligent than me, who fall into the trap of staying. They are ridiculously stressed. They may have a couple more zeros in their bank account, but the cost to their personal lives, to what they wanted to do, to their health, to their sanity, and to what they could have created next, was massive. A smaller amount, proportionally, is much better, and for a founder who has sold a company it is still a decent amount of money.

Open source makes leaving easier. If the work you care about is open, you take it with you, which is what open source what you build argued: the one way to keep using your own work after you leave.

What the research says

Losing the top job early is the norm, not the exception. Noam Wasserman studied 212 American start-ups for the Harvard Business Review in 2008: by the time they were three years old, half of the founders were no longer CEO; in year four, 40% were still in the post; and fewer than a quarter led their companies' initial public offerings. "Four out of five entrepreneurs," he wrote, "are forced to step down from the CEO's post."

His conclusion was that founders choose, knowingly or not, between being rich and being king: they "make 'rich' versus 'king' trade-offs to maximize either their wealth or their control over the company", in the words of the article's abstract. Giving up control tends to build a more valuable company; keeping it tends to mean a smaller one. Neither is wrong. What hurts is not knowing which one you chose.

Acquisitions follow the same pattern. A study of 4,000 acquisitions of US tech start-ups by J. Daniel Kim of Wharton, reported by MIT Sloan, found that 33% of acquired workers left in the first year, against 12% of ordinary hires. People who built something often do not stay to be managed inside someone else's.

The Mourinho clause

I call the answer the Mourinho clause, after José Mourinho, the football manager.

My theory about him, and it is a theory, is this. He had great success early, and he realised that what he is very good at is impact: coming into a club, winning something it has not won before, and changing it. So he goes to a club and says: I will come in, I will get you success, I will be here for two years, maybe three, and when my impact wanes and it is time to part, here are the terms. The cynical version is that the friction at the end is part of the plan, so that the club finds it easy to let him go. I do not know that, and nothing I found shows it.

What the record does show is that the exits were priced in advance. When he left Real Madrid by mutual agreement in 2013, he gave up, as AFP reported, "the €20 million ($26 million) compensation clause in the event he was sacked", and the club gave up "a clause of the same amount" payable if he left for another club: the exit was priced both ways before either side needed it. Manchester United's dismissal of him in 2018 cost the club £19.6m in compensation to him and his staff. And his contract at Benfica reportedly carried a €15m termination clause, the price at which Real Madrid hired him back this year. His third seasons have been hard often enough that Sky Sports named the pattern "third-season syndrome".

I think most senior executives do the same these days, once they have enough power and someone who wants them: they negotiate the exit on the way in. Founders should too.

Negotiate the exit at the start

The time to agree the exit is at the beginning, when the investors want you, not at the end, when the board does not. Four things are worth writing down.

  • The trigger. What counts as losing control: a new board majority, a change of control, a sale, the strategy no longer yours.
  • The terms. That leaving then makes you a good leaver. In UK shareholder agreements, as Ashfords explains, good leavers are typically "those leaving due to reasons beyond their control", such as illness or redundancy, and keep their vested shares or are paid fair market value; bad leavers, typically "those dismissed for cause", generally forfeit their unvested shares. Leaving because you lost control is not on the usual good-leaver list, which is exactly why it should be written in. Add accelerated vesting, a price for your shares, and a fixed transition period.
  • What you keep. Your name, your reputation, the open source you wrote, and a non-compete narrow enough to let you build again.
  • Your number. See below.

This is also leverage in the negotiation. If investors know that asking for too much control means losing the founder, they price that in. Sometimes they will still ask for it, and that may be the right outcome for both sides: the company has matured, it no longer needs that ball of energy, it needs someone to execute, and a new CEO and a new CTO take it from there. That might be the right outcome for everybody, and it is easiest when it was agreed at the start.

Write your number down now

Write down, today, the amount you would be happy with: the number that would change your life, written while you are broke or close to it. Then, when the alternatives arrive, the next round, the bigger offer, the reason to stay three more years, look back at that number. You will probably find you passed it some time ago. Take a step back, and be happy with what you have.

This connects to the previous article. The people who control a company decide how big each slice is and when it is sold. Selling a slice is not an exit; selling control is. A founder who understands the difference can sell slices for years, as sell, don't issue argued, and choose the day they sell the part that matters.

What this does not claim

  • The Mourinho theory is mine. He has not said it; the clauses are documented, the motives are not.
  • Some figures are as reported. The Benfica clause and his later appointment are from press reports.
  • Every company is different. Some founders are the right people to run the company for decades.
  • This is not legal advice. Leaver and control terms need a lawyer, in your jurisdiction.
  • Wasserman's data are from 2008. The pattern is old; the numbers may have moved.
