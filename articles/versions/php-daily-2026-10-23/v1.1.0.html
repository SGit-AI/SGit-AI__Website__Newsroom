Why this hour {#why-this-hour}

I want one of these a day, a fixed snapshot rather than a running commentary, so that anyone can go back and see where things stood. For 23 October the best moment is 23:30 UTC, when the conductor agent refreshed the team's status page, reading all twenty agent sessions, the repository and the scheduled tasks without asking any agent. Everything below is as of that snapshot unless it says otherwise; a few things that landed in the hour after are marked as such.

The agents run on a schedule {#scheduled}

Yesterday I wrote about this workflow as almost working. By the end of the day it was further along than that article said, and I should correct it here: I read the schedule vault, which the agents had not yet adopted, and the agents were already running without it.

• The scheduled curator, on Sonnet, with its key from the QR handshake, woke at its five times and promoted 33 captures from the source watch's queue: the evidence vault reached 260 sources, with 28 new claims, six of them disputed. 164 queue rows were still waiting, at about 18 a wake.

• The scheduled source watch became active at 07:00, 09:30, 12:00, 14:30 and 17:00 London time. Its evening pass was on its third attempt at 23:23 UTC, after two container restarts lost the first two, which is exactly the failure I described.

• Just after midnight, the schedules themselves moved out of the schedule vault and into the repository: each scheduled agent now has a card with its objective, its wake times and its wake prompt word for word, and a run record for every wake.

Pack mode {#pack-mode}

The source watch had grown to the point where it hurt: over a gigabyte, slow to clone and slow to pull. The researcher measured pack mode, the same vault stored as packs rather than as individual objects: a fresh clone in 95 seconds against 13 minutes and 47 seconds, the whole history pushed in 96 seconds, and the normal vault's object listing now timing out on the server. With two writers on the pack vault, a pull of the scheduled session's commit took 176 seconds and a one-file correction was pushed in 77. The watch moved into its pack-mode copy, 7r83qic5, and the scheduled curator followed it. The curator's first session advised against moving the evidence vault yet: a pack vault does not open in the browser, and the site's vault pages need that.

The site watches itself {#telemetry}

In a day and a half the dev desk built the observability I had been asking for. Every session on the site is now a reader, the editor, a power user or team member, a synthetic reader, or a crawler, declared by the browser where it can be and inferred before that. The telemetry app, v0.4.0 by the evening, makes every chart and every cell a filter, colours by any breakdown, and draws journeys as a Sankey and as a page network. Its stats are in a public vault, 0cfkdcvh, updated at every drain. The first thing it showed was uncomfortable and useful: the front page leaks. In the journeys, 134 sessions ended after their first page.

The risk graph as a tool {#risk-graph}

A collaborator's ideas (CI-002) turned the risk graph from a picture into a tool. Every edge now says how it is known: read from a capture, read from the fix commit, or inferred by an agent, and the inferred ones are drawn dashed and can be hidden. The fix commit's 270 tests are nodes. You can start from one thing, a CVE, a risk, a file, a test, and follow it one step at a time, or climb from a file to the risks and controls above it. And a gaps page is computed at every build. On 23 October it said: no control is joined to code or a test, no code has an owner, and 14 risks have no story. That is the kind of honest gap list I want every system to have.

A site that watches its own readers {#telemetry}

The biggest change of the day was on the newsroom's side. In a day and a half, the dev desk agent built the tools I had been asking for: a way to see who reads the site and what works on it, without tracking anyone.

Private by design. Every page sends one anonymous event: the page, the site that referred the reader, a country and city from a lookup, the language, the kind of screen and a random id that lives in that browser tab. No IP address, no cookie, no name. The event is encrypted in the reader's browser before it leaves, and sent to a write-only lane on a private vault: the lane accepts writes and answers every one the same way, so nobody can read anything back through it. Only the holder of the vault can read the events.

Not everyone who opens a page is a reader. The first night looked busy: 356 page views from one session. It was Google's crawler, working through 252 pages. So every session is now in one of five groups: readers, the editor, power users and the team, synthetic visitors (agents and tests, including our own), and crawlers. Of the 1,093 page views by 20:26 UTC, crawlers were 565 and readers 233. Without the groups, every chart would have been mostly Google.

[figure dy-telemetry-who.webp] All 1,093 page views by group: 233 by readers in 168 sessions, 110 by the editor, 9 by power users and the team, 176 synthetic, 565 by crawlers. | screenshot

[figure dy-telemetry-timeline.webp] Page views per hour from 13:00 on 22 October to 20:00 on 23 October, by group: the crawler working through the night, readers steadiest in the UK morning and afternoon. | screenshot

A public face and a private dashboard. The counts, and only the counts, go into a public vault anyone can open, with a world map of where readers are. The full events stay private, in a dashboard that opens straight from its own vault and works like Kibana: click any bar, country, cell or node to keep only that; shift-click to leave it out; every number follows.

[figure dy-telemetry-map.webp] Readers by country, on a log scale: eighteen countries, the United Kingdom first by far, then the United States and Norway. | screenshot

What it found: the front page leaks. The journeys view follows sessions page by page. 113 reader visits started on the front page, and 81% of them read nothing else; the front page is read most and sends the fewest readers on. Of the sites that send readers, kode24.no, a Norwegian developer site, sends ones who stay: more than a third read a second page, against one in five from LinkedIn. That is the kind of finding a newsroom needs on day two, not in month three.

How it was built in a day and a half. This is the part I find most powerful. The dev desk did not build a telemetry platform. It assembled one from pieces we had already made into commodities: encrypted vaults that keep their history; write-only append lanes that let a browser send encrypted rows to a vault it cannot read; keys that live inside the vault, so whoever can open it can process it from the browser with no server; and apps that live in a vault of their own, versioned there, and open the data where it sits. No database, no analytics vendor, no server to run. When the pieces are commodities, a capability like this is a day and a half of conversation with an agent, not a project.

The vaults, and a 13-minute download that became 95 seconds {#vaults}

For readers new to how this newsroom works: the PHP site is rebuilt from vaults, never written by hand. A vault is an encrypted, versioned folder, like a git repository whose server cannot read it, made with our open source tool, sgit. Nineteen vaults sit behind the site: the evidence, with every source and its capture; the research agent's source watch, every page it follows with a snapshot whenever it changes; the private telemetry; and others. Two are public, and anyone can open them read-only in a browser.

The source watch is the biggest. By 23 October it held 63,662 objects and 1.6 GB, growing by about 5,000 objects a pass, and it was failing: the server could no longer list it, a fresh copy took 13 minutes and 47 seconds to download, and one push needed 12 attempts. Moved to sgit's new pack mode, which stores each push as one zip, the same history downloaded in 95 seconds and pushed in 96. The full story of how pack mode came to exist the day before it was needed is in a tangent that shipped.
