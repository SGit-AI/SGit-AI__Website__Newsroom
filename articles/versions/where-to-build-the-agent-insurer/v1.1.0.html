2 unchanged paragraphs, under The question, and why it has more than one answer {#question}

I think the honest answer is that it hascan have more than one home, because it does more than one job. A managing agent prices and sells policies on an insurer's behalf and carries no risk on its own balance sheet; it needs a licence to distribute insurance and a carrier willing to lend its capital. An insurer pays the claims and has to hold capital against a bad year. Distribution needs licences where the customers are. Those three jobs are regulated differently in each place, and the best place for one is not the best place for the others.

7 unchanged paragraphs, under Sell into the US {#us}, The EU, second {#eu}

Carry the risk in Bermuda {#bermuda}

When the loss record exists and the company is ready to carry risk, I would do it in Bermuda. Its regime has a class for this stage: a Class 3A "Small commercial insurer" holds capital set by a risk-based formula, with "a $1 million floor" (BMA). It has a class for an insurer that "intends to carry on general business in an innovative manner", and a sandbox for "a company aiming to carry on general business in an innovative and experimental manner"; cyber insurers have used both, and one, Breach Insurance, moved from the sandbox to the innovative class in 2023 (BMA). And it is where risk capital meets insurance: the Bermuda Stock Exchange listed "more than 93% of global issuance" of insurance-linked securities in 2025, including "new cyber catastrophe bonds" (Insurance Business, 11 February 2026). Once agent risk has a record, that is the market that will take its tail.

The UK is the alternative, with the PRA's mobilisation regime and the same Solvency-style discipline; and an insurer that wants EU clients directly may need an EU carrier as well. But for a first balance sheet, Bermuda's floor and its capital markets make it the place I would look first.

The Middle East, for capital first {#gulf}

The Gulf has new regimes and a great deal of money, and I think its first role is the money. As an insurance market, its entry costs differ widely: in Saudi Arabia an insurer's paid-up capital "shall not be less than three hundred million Saudi riyals" under the law as amended in 2021; in Abu Dhabi, the ADGM's captive rules started from US$150,000, and its regulator proposed in December 2025 that insurers hold "120% of the ASCR under normal circumstances" (Reinsurance News). We could not read the DIFC regulator's own pages, so its figures are from secondary sources and are left out here.

As a source of capital it is a different story: the region's funds invest in balance sheets of exactly the kind the third stage needs, and the ADGM's regulator is rewriting its insurance rules as it does so. I would raise there, and sell there later.

Carry the risk in London too {#carry}

When the company is ready to carry risk itself, I would do that in London as well, so that the underwriting and the balance sheet sit under the same supervisors and the same market. There are two ways in. The first is a UK insurer: the PRA has run a mobilisation regime for new insurers since 31 December 2024, "to facilitate new insurer expansion and entry into the UK market", an optional stage of up to twelve months with restrictions that begins at authorisation (Bank of England), and a general insurer's minimum capital floor is £2.4 million. The second is capital at Lloyd's: "A Lloyd's syndicate is formed by one or more members joining together to provide capital and accept insurance risks on a several liability basis", and the members' capital is held as Funds at Lloyd's (Lloyd's). A company that starts as a coverholder already works inside that market, so putting its own capital behind its own book is a short step.

An insurer that wants EU clients directly may need an EU carrier as well, under Solvency II's €2.7 million floor. That can wait for the second market.

The Gulf, for capital {#gulf}

The Gulf has new regimes and a great deal of money, and I think its role here is the money. The UAE's two financial centres are both reworking their insurance rules: in Abu Dhabi, the ADGM's regulator proposed in December 2025 that insurers hold "120% of the ASCR under normal circumstances" (Reinsurance News); the DIFC's own pages could not be read, so its figures are left out. The region's funds invest in balance sheets of exactly the kind the third job needs. I would raise there, and leave selling there for later.

2 unchanged paragraphs, under How much it would take {#money}

Stage one, two years as a managing agent: about £4.35 million. A team of eight in the first year and fifteen in the second, at £150,000 each including everything, and £450,000 a year for regulatory, legal, actuarial, professional indemnity and technology costs: £1.65 million in the first year and £2.7 million in the second. No capital backs the policies, because the carrier's does. That is a seed or Series A, and the market is funding it: insurtech funding was "US$5.08 billion" in 2025 (Insurance Journal, 9 March 2026, citing Gallagher Re), and in the first quarter of 2026 "InsurTechs linked to AI liability and cyber insurance attracted USD 444.84 million" (Reinsurance News, 7 May 2026). AIUC has raised $55 million in two rounds, and Harbor was reported this month to have raised a $10 million seed.

Stage two, carrying the risk: the capital follows from the limits sold. This is where the deterministic policy pays off. Because every row is capped and every unnamed capability excluded, the worst year can be bounded from the limits on the book: take all the limits sold, the share of them one shared failure could hit at once (one model's flaw, one tool's bug, every agent built on it), keep half after reinsurance, and add a 20% buffer.

So my opinion: raise about £5 million to start, which is stage one with some headroom; plan a second raise of £5 million to £10 million to carry the first two thousand agents, from investors who fund balance sheets; and by ten thousand agents, the capital is a reinsurance and capital markets question, which is where Bermuda comes in. The share hit at once is the number that matters most, and it is the one with no loss data behind it yet: it is why the first books should be small and spread across models and tools.

Running the company: about £4.35 million for two years. A team of eight in the first year and fifteen in the second, at £150,000 each including everything, and £450,000 a year for regulatory, legal, actuarial, professional indemnity and technology costs: £1.65 million in the first year and £2.7 million in the second. The market is funding companies like this: insurtech funding was "US$5.08 billion" in 2025 (Insurance Journal, 9 March 2026, citing Gallagher Re), and in the first quarter of 2026 "InsurTechs linked to AI liability and cyber insurance attracted USD 444.84 million" (Reinsurance News, 7 May 2026). AIUC has raised $55 million in two rounds, and Harbor was reported this month to have raised a $10 million seed.

Backing the policies: what the rest of the money buys is cover. This is where the deterministic policy pays off. Because every row is capped and every unnamed capability excluded, the worst year can be bounded from the limits on the book: take all the limits sold, the share of them one shared failure could hit at once (one model's flaw, one tool's bug, every agent built on it), keep half after reinsurance, and add a 20% buffer. Turn that round, and every pound of capital raised above the running costs sets a ceiling on the cover the company can sell. So rather than raising in stages, the company can raise once, for the cover it intends to sell from the start, and the size of the raise decides that ceiling.

With £80,000 of limits per agent, as in the worked example, and two years of running costs taken out first:

| Raised in one go | Capital backing policies | If 5% can be hit at once | If 10% can be hit at once | |---|---|---|---| | £10 million | £5.65 million | about £188 million of cover, 2,354 agents | about £94 million of cover, 1,177 agents | | £50 million | £45.65 million | about £1.52 billion of cover, 19,020 agents | about £761 million of cover, 9,510 agents |

Both are above the UK's £2.4 million floor. At the worked example's rate, £6,720 a year for £80,000 of cover with five enforced controls, the £10 million book could earn about £7.9 million to £15.8 million of premium a year, and the £50 million book about £64 million to £128 million; those are ceilings at full use of the capital, not forecasts.

So my opinion: decide the cover first, and raise for it in one go. £10 million is enough to prove the model on about a thousand to two thousand agents; £50 million is the raise for a company that means to insure ten to twenty thousand agents from the start, and to be the place the large providers come to. The share hit at once is the number that matters most, and it is the one with no loss data behind it yet: it is why even a large book should start spread across models and tools.
