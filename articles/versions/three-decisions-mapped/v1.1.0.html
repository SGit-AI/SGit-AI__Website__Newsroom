Summary: The fourth article in the series on agent decision policies puts the pieces together on three scenarios, two of them real. First, deleting an agent's memory in Mondweep Chakravorty's SGit adapter for Ruflo: the behaviour row is defined, implemented and enforced, the decision behind its "approval" is not yet defined, and nothing records what happened. Second, this newsroom's own release, which ships on its own: the behaviour boundary holds at the platform, the workflow is a script and a validator, and the interesting part is a decision nobody wrote down. In every release since this afternoon I shipped with one failing test that predates my changes, and I made that call each time on my own. It should be a written decision with an owner and an expiry. The same evening produced two small cases for the case-law article: a figure that shipped with a date nobody had checked, and a counter that flagged two commits without a leak scan, both of which turned out to be in scratch test repositories, a case the rule did not mean to cover. Third, an illustrative customer refund built on the third design in Hope or enforcement, where the limits are already in the tools, and a new workflow that tries to widen them is caught by a subset check before it runs. Each element is mapped with the policy it belongs to and fourfive states: defined, implemented, enforcedenforced, observed and observed.verified.

Behaviour and workflow. Both are mature, because they are built into the tools: the agent calls propose_refund, the tool checks the limit, the refund is made or refused, and it is recorded. There is no path around it, which is the lesson the policy guards the door draws from the Ruflo run.

Decision. Above 100 GBP a person decides, and the routing is defined. What is only partly there is the part that turns a person's approval into evidence: what they saw, why they agreed, and whether the next similar case can follow it.

Behaviour and workflow. Both are enforced, because they are built into the tools: the agent calls propose_refund, the tool checks the limit, the refund is made or refused, and it is recorded. There is no path around it, which is the lesson the policy guards the door draws from the Ruflo run.

The amount nobody checks. Under 100 GBP, the tool checks that the refund is within the limit, and nothing checks that it is the right amount. An agent that misreads a discount and refunds 90 GBP where 70 was paid passes every control here. That is the failure in Ashish Rajan's Northstar example, and allowed is not right answers it: the amount needs a decision policy of its own, worked out from the order records by something other than the agent. The Northstar twin runs that on thirteen cases.

Decision above the limit. Above 100 GBP a person decides, and the routing is defined. What is only partly there is the part that turns a person's approval into evidence: what they saw, why they agreed, and whether the next similar case can follow it.
