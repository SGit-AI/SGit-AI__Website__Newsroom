2 unchanged paragraphs, under No wave, and nothing confirmed {#record}

The PHP story site's evidence on compromise is a list of every statement, each with its captured source. Its line, at 09:43 UTC on 27 October, six days in: "No named organisation has said it was compromised through php2shell." Its 53 rows divide as the figure shows. Three kinds of evidence need keeping apart here: attempts, seen by honeypots and sensors; successful exploitation in a lab or honeypot set up for it, such as the 35 shells below; and a confirmed compromise of a production system, of which there is none so far. CISA's listing sits between them: CISA's own assessment, added to both CVE records, marks exploitation as "active", and names no victim. The eight named organisations are the "reports of individual breaches": GMO Internet Group found malicious programs on servers of its trading service and has not stated the cause; Czech Technical University stopped an attack; Valtec first blamed the PHP flaw, then said the attackers used a flaw in its own service; DMM halted services and would not say whether it was PHP; Germany's Interior Ministry said its site "was not hacked"; Anexia confirmed attempts; Ahold Delhaize denied any link; and the Square Enix report is second-hand and does not say how the access happened.

2 unchanged paragraphs, under Is AI driving it? {#ai}

Jeremiah's opinion is that nothing in the record shows AI driving up how many vulnerabilities adversaries exploit, or how fast. The yearly numbers are consistent with it, with one caveat. From 1 January to 26 October, NVD published 89,531 CVEs this year, more than twice the 40,266 of the same period in 2025. CISA's catalogue added 274 entries, 30% more than the 210 of 2025. Whatever is driving the flood of disclosures, much of it plausibly AI-assisted discovery, the evidenced exploitation is growing much more slowly: the rate fell from 0.62% of publications in 2023 to 0.31% this year. The caveat is that 30% is still growth, that CISA's additions include older CVEs, and that neither number says what caused it. "How fast" needs a different measure, the time from publication to first exploitation, which we have asked for below.

Jeremiah's opinion is that nothing in the record shows AI driving up how many vulnerabilities adversaries exploit, or how fast. The yearly numbers describe the background, but they cannot test that. From 1 January to 26 October, NVD published 89,531 CVEs this year, more than twice the 40,266 of the same period in 2025; CISA's catalogue added 274 entries, 30% more than the 210 of 2025, so additions as a share of publications fell from 0.62% in 2023 to 0.31%. The two numbers count different populations, since CISA's additions include CVEs published in earlier years; the catalogue records what CISA has evidence for, not attack volume or speed; and neither says anything about AI. Testing the claim needs matched cohorts: the CVEs published in a period, each followed for the same time, with the time to the first evidence of exploitation. We have asked for that below.

Three questions need keeping apart. Can AI help build exploits? Yes, by the account of a team that found php2shell: Wiz's researchers wrote that frontier models enabled them "to develop and adapt working exploits across multiple applications in hours", and that "a single researcher completed in a week what previously would have taken a world-class team months of work". Are adversaries using it at scale? Jeremiah expects so: "I can't imagine any exploit writer or cyber adversary not using heavy AI tooling at this point". Has it produced more exploited flaws, faster, or more victims? That is the post's claim, and on this record the answer is not yet, with the caveat that four days and a catalogue are a small window on it. The first is a demonstrated research capability; the post is about the second and third.

Reviewed, and corrected {#review}

The editor asked ChatGPT to review this article on 27 October, and pasted its review to me. Its verdict was "changes needed", with nine findings, and I agree with all nine. The record is on the reviews page, with what was done about each. In short:

1. EPSS was used as if it were an observation. It is a 30-day forecast; the summary now says "expected", and the window section says what a low score does and does not mean. 2. The yearly comparison was used about AI. It now describes the background and says what a test would need. 3. Wiz's account was missing. It is now quoted, with three questions kept apart: research capability, adversaries' use, and victims. 4. "0.9998" did not reproduce both scores. Now 0.99997 and 0.99981, or "above 0.999". 5. The absence of a confirmed victim had lost its bound in the summaries. It now carries its date, and attempts, lab or honeypot success and production compromise are kept apart, with CISA's "Exploitation: active" on the CVE records. 6. 1,448 against 1,394 was "explained" when it was not. Now "not reconciled". 7. VulnCheck's 25 matched in count only. Assignment dates and KEV membership are now marked as not checked. 8. The attention chart was presented as controlled. It is now labelled as descriptive. 9. The fall from 36.7 to 32 million was read as patching. Now with the other possible causes, and Fortinet's note on backports.

One point in the review I could not confirm: that watchTowr reports exploitation through its honeypot network. The PHP story site's capture tool got a 403 from watchTowr's pages, and its fact-check desk marked that claim as resting on search summaries only. The core of Jeremiah's post is unchanged by any of this: no wave of confirmed breaches in four days, and patch what adversaries exploit.
