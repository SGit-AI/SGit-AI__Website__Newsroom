## The book

[Generation Z Developers](https://leanpub.com/generation-z), subtitled "Key concepts and ideas for the next generation of developers", is on Leanpub at 60% complete, 136 pages and 30,695 words, last updated on 8 October 2018, with 465 readers. It is also on Amazon, and its source is on GitHub, in [Book_Generation_Z_Developer](https://github.com/DinisCruz/Book_Generation_Z_Developer). I wrote it the way it told readers to work: in Markdown, in Git, in public, published early and often, with GitHub issues for feedback.

It was a decent book. It was also full of spelling mistakes, and a lot of it was never finished.

## What it said

It opened with why I was worried about Generation Z, "the generation born after 1996": not their values, which I liked, but that, having grown up with technology that simply works, many had missed the history of how it came to work. And it had one chapter that I still give, almost word for word, to people looking for a job:

The best way to get a job is to have ONE competitive advantage. One activity or task that you can do better than the person or company hiring you.

For a lot of companies in the 1990s, it said, that was simply using a computer, and in the 2000s, using the internet. Then the advice: "Act like you are part of the company", by collaborating on its open-source projects, which is [act as the job you want](act-as-the-job-you-want.md), eight years early.

On AI, it said: "One of the most important areas that you need to gain a strong understanding in the next 5 years is Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence (AI)." ChatGPT arrived four years later.

## What held, and what did not

**What held.** The competitive-advantage argument is exactly [hired for the workflow](hired-for-the-workflow.md): today the one advantage is running agentic workflows. Act like you are part of the company. Publish, publish, publish. GitHub as your CV. Write for your future self. Pioneers, settlers and town planners, which became [software vendors as villagers](software-vendors-as-villagers.md) and [explorer coding](explorer-coding.md).

**What did not.** The book's list of technologies to learn, Jira, WallabyJs, AWS's machine-learning services, has dated, as such lists do. Its AI chapter pointed readers at cloud machine-learning tools to learn "how to program it"; the bigger shift turned out to be learning how to use it, and how to direct agents, which is a different skill from building models. And its view of who is a developer was narrower than it should have been: today anyone who can brief an agent to build something is one.

The most interesting thing in the repository is what is not finished. Of its 138 files, 90 are outlines: a title, "Topics to cover and ideas", and a few bullet points. Life patterns: 21 of 27 are outlines. Concepts: 26 of 27. Security: all 8. Your CV: 8 of 9.

## The name

The first question was whether to call it Generation Z Developers, or Generation AI Developers, or Generation Z professionals or workers. I like the word developer, and I think it is more right now than in 2018, because this generation all have to be developers: they all have to create. The first book said they needed to know Git and version control, spreadsheets, automation, even Slack. The problem was that there was no easy way in. I was giving advice, and the bar of entry was very high. Now it is very low. You could even give this book to Claude and say: teach this person, give them the workflows, one at a time.

And the people I want to give it to, those coming out of university or looking for their first or next job, are still Generation Z: the oldest were 22 when I wrote it, and are 30 now. It is not only for them. A target helps, and it also tells everyone else: learn this, or be outcompeted by the people who have. Generation Z is the generation entering the job market now, and in Europe in particular I think they are being fed a lot of fear about AI, and are not embracing it the way they should.

So: keep the title, and mark the new edition in brackets: *Generation Z Developers (AI edition)*. It says what changed in two words, and it reads as an edition of the same book, not a different one. And date it, October 2026, because what it describes is the agentic workflow as I run it now, which will change.

## Explorers, villagers and town planners

If everyone is a developer, the better question is what kind. Simon Wardley's answer, first as [pioneers, settlers and town planners](https://blog.gardeviance.org/2015/03/on-pioneers-settlers-town-planners-and.html) and since 2023 as [explorers, villagers and town planners](https://blog.gardeviance.org/2023/12/how-to-organise-yourself-dangerous-path.html), gives the book its frame: the explorer who builds something new and rough, the villager who makes it work for others, the town planner who turns it into something everyone can rely on. Where do you fit, and where do you want to? The 2018 book had this as an outline, and mentioned Wardley mapping once. This edition can explain Wardley maps properly, because it is now something people starting out need to know, and it makes a good narrative to hang the visualisations and infographics on.

But this one probably has enough content and material already, and because one version is already published, we can build on the back of it: fix it, do a much better release, and publish it as a new version on Amazon. It is a good target: something I can focus on, iterate on quickly, release, and move on. If the workflow works, a new version of Hacking Portugal can follow the same way.

And it is the book I would like to give people. Every time I meet someone and ask: do you know about Wardley maps? Do you have an agentic workflow? I would like to be able to hand them this.
