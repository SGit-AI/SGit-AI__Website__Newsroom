## The problem: legal is everywhere, and founders are new to it

This follows [the jobs that never scaled](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/jobs-that-never-scaled.html), applied to the law. Having someone who understands the legal side in the conversation is critical in almost every part of a startup: shares and the cap table, voting rights, intellectual property, contracts with customers, human resources, data protection, and above all the investment. And most founders have little experience of any of it. They don't know which questions to ask, what a cap table will look like after two rounds, or what a participating preference does to them in a sale. They often don't have good lawyers, or any, or not ones who know the particular thing that matters.

So they get worse deals than they should. Sometimes it isn't even malicious: if you hold the power, why wouldn't you ask for more? But the question I keep asking, as in [the controllable founder](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/the-controllable-founder.html), [the exit is when you sell control](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/the-exit-is-when-you-sell-control.html) and [sell, don't issue](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/sell-dont-issue.html), is why an investor wouldn't align its terms with what the founder is happy with, rather than with what it can get. Part of the answer is that the founder can't argue well enough. That part can be fixed.

## Why legal never scaled, and why it can now

Legal services never scaled because they were expensive, and they were expensive because of the time: onboarding, reading, checking, re-reading the next draft, a lot of repetitive work that the best people did not want to do, and that clients paid senior rates for anyway.

Models change exactly that part. I have used ChatGPT and Claude for real legal work, quite successfully, and here is what people miss: even when they hallucinate, they are very good at reading every detail and picking up inconsistencies. Three things I have done that worked:

- **Mirror terms.** If a contract asks me for a particular level of indemnity, or a particular term, ask for the same on the other side. Terms and conditions became much fairer that way.
- **"Here is my understanding."** When I am not fully confident, because I am not a lawyer, I write to the other side's lawyers: here is my understanding of what this clause does, do you agree? If they say it says X and the contract says twice X, that is now their problem, not mine, and if the text changes halfway through, it is misleading. Those things matter in a relationship, and especially at the final due diligence.
- **Infographics of terms.** Turning terms and conditions into a picture shows what they actually ask.

And when the terms are right, the models agree with each other. Ask ChatGPT and Claude separately, and when they both say "this now covers your bases", it usually does. They are also very good at finding blind spots. None of this replaces a lawyer. It replaces the part of a lawyer's time that should never have cost what it did.

## The service

Two teams of people and agents, one on each side, and the deal room where they meet.

So here is the service. A legal desk for startup funding, where agentic workflows drive the efficiency of the humans inside them. This is not a human in the loop, approving what a machine did. It is agents doing the reading, the extraction, the mirroring, the cap table models, the plain-language explanations and the checking, so that each lawyer's hour goes on judgement, negotiation and signature.

The founder does as much as they can themselves, with the platform: depending on their skills and their time, more or less. The platform tunes its prompts and workflows, not its models; fine-tuning a model is the last thing you should do. And the founder can always go back to their own Claude or ChatGPT and ask "what do you think of this?", which a good service should welcome.

The commercial model follows from the economics. With agents doing the repetitive work, and lawyers working virtually with low overheads, the service can charge a fair rate for an hour, half a day or a day of a lawyer's time, plus a price per task. A monthly plan you can cancel any month is fine. A yearly subscription makes no sense for this: it is a SaaS model that suits the vendor's recurring revenue, not the client. Better still, [pay per use](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/paying-after-the-value.html), which I would argue is also better for the platform.

## Three markets: the founder, the investor, and both

**The founder's side.** Counsel paid by the founder, on the founder's team, who understands the intricacies, and who doesn't cost a ridiculous amount of money. Investors benefit too: by the time they arrive, the data room is in order and the due diligence has largely been done.

**The investor's side.** I would argue investors need this even more. The difference is between someone working in a spreadsheet and email, and someone with an agentic workflow that can read a whole data room, collect everything, raise the questions and draft the memo in hours. Investment is a competitive sport: as a founder, I want an investor who works at this speed, and an investor who works at analogue speed will lose deals to one who doesn't.

**Both, in one room.** Each side goes to its own team, agents plus humans, and says: this is what I want, these are my terms, this is my situation. The two teams hash it out. And who pays? I would want the investor to pay for its own diligence: why should a founder pay to answer questions for an investor who may not make an offer? Whoever pays the bill will have the service, and even the models, a little more aligned with them. That is fine, as long as each side has its own.

## Sign everything, and keep the intent

The part that makes this more than faster drafting is the signing. With a public key infrastructure, every party has a key pair, and everything that matters is signed: the founder's principles, the term sheet, each clause, each version, each "here is our understanding" and its confirmation. A signed clause cannot change without the signature failing, and every change between versions is a diff with a name on it.

I also think contracts should become a graph, what I call a fractal document: the intent at the top, the principles under it, then the terms, then the clauses, then the plain-language versions in each language. You sign off the intent and the principles; the legal clauses are the way to enforce them. Across languages and cultures, the plain principles travel better than the legal text. And you could argue that a clause that contradicts the signed principles should not be valid: that is a proposal, and the wording still has to be right, but it is the direction.

We did an early version of this in [a vault for risk acceptance](https://sgit.ai/demos/vaults/risk-acceptance/index.html), before vaults had append lanes. What can be built now is a different order of thing.

## Publish your terms

The last part is where I am going with this myself. I am going to raise, on the terms I want, and because everything I do is open, I am going to publish them: the term sheet, what I want, what I am interested in, and the materials. Then I can tell investors: hire this kind of service, or use your own, and debate it with mine until you reach a conclusion. Ask questions, find things, argue. But here are the terms, and this is my line.

That scales for me, because it is a page on my site. I have done versions of this before, for The Cyber Boardroom and for MyFeeds, but I never had a product with real demand. Now I do, which is what makes the conversation with investors interesting.

## See it working: three vaults

To make this concrete, I asked the agents to build it. ## The business, and who should build it

The features such a service needs, against what already exists in open source today.

There is a lot of commercial value here. A service like this has three markets, founders, investors and the deal between them, and a cost structure that legal services never had: agents for the repetitive work, lawyers for the judgement, no office, no retainer. Someone should start this company, or invest in it, and existing legal-tech companies would do well to measure themselves against these features.

And you don't need to go much further than what exists today. Vaults are data rooms, with history and read keys. Append lanes carry messages between the sides. Vault apps are the applications each side uses, with no server. Key pairs and [signed records](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/pki-everywhere.html) are built, and QR codes carry signatures and sealed messages. Agent teams with cards, roles and loops are [running every day](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/what-an-agent-is.html). All of it is open source and runs on the client side, which is exactly what you want for documents nobody else should hold. The fractal agreement and binding understandings work in the demonstration; the meter is [a published design](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/the-user-keeps-the-meter.html). Companies in this space should look at it and ask what they can reuse.

## What this does not claim

- **Not legal advice,** and not a substitute for a lawyer: the point is to make the lawyer's time count.
- **Models make mistakes.** The workflow is built around checking them, with humans who sign.
- **A signature is not a legal contract by itself.** Whether a signed clause binds depends on the law and the agreement; the demonstration shows the mechanism.
- **"A clause that contradicts its principles should be void" is a proposal.** It would need careful wording and a willing court.
- **The demonstration is made up,** scripted, and its three vaults compute their hashes separately, so the same version shows different roots in each.
- **The prices and comparisons are illustrations,** with their assumptions shown in the vaults.
