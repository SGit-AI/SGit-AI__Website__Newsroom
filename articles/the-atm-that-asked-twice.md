## Three prices for the same 200 euros

On 17 November 2026 I withdrew 200 euros at a cash machine in Portugal with my UK card. Here are the three prices for the same money:

- **The ECB reference rate,** the latest available, 16 November: 0.83450 pounds per euro, so 166.90 pounds.
- **My bank,** which did the conversion when I refused the machine's: 167.12 pounds, within 0.1% of the ECB rate.
- **The machine's "guaranteed" rate:** 0.94840 pounds per euro, so 189.68 pounds, 13.6% above the ECB rate and 22.56 pounds more than my bank charged.

The whole cost sits in the machine's offer.

## What the screens said

The first screen: 200 euros at 0.94840, 189.68 pounds, accept without or with conversion, beside a Union Jack.

The first screen showed the withdrawal, 200,00 EUR, the machine's rate and the total in pounds, and two buttons: "accept without conversion" on the left, and "accept with conversion" on the right, beside a Union Jack.

The second screen asked again: exchange rate determined by your bank, or a fixed exchange rate guaranteed.

When I chose the euro, a second screen asked me the same question again: "exchange rate will be determined by your bank" on one side, "fixed exchange rate guaranteed" on the other.

## Where the money goes

Two paths: accept, and the ATM's service keeps the mark-up; decline, and my bank converts near the ECB rate.

If you accept, the machine's conversion service turns your euros into pounds at its own rate and keeps the difference, a margin usually shared between the conversion company and whoever hosts the machine. If you decline, the euros go through the card network to your own bank, which converts close to the reference rate. I have not established who runs the conversion on this particular machine, or how the margin is split.

The "guarantee" protects against almost nothing. A cash withdrawal settles within a day or two, and a card issuer's rate normally sits close to the reference rate. A Norwegian bank that looked at 1,500 of its customers' withdrawals abroad found taking the home currency was cheaper in only 4 of them; across Europe, Stiftung Warentest found mark-ups from 2.6% to 12%. This one was above that range.

## What the law says

The EU regulated this in Regulation (EU) 2019/518, which added Article 3a to the regulation on cross-border payments. Anyone offering currency conversion at a cash machine or a till must express the total charge as a percentage mark-up over the latest ECB euro reference rate, and show it before the payment starts. That has applied since April 2020, and since April 2021 card issuers must also message their customers with the mark-up after such a payment.

The EU chose transparency, not a ban, even though BEUC, the European consumer organisation, had called this "the great currency conversion scam". On the two screens I photographed, there is a rate and a total, but no percentage mark-up. I did not photograph every screen, so I cannot say none showed it; if none did, the display does not appear to meet Article 3a.

## Six levers on two screens

Six levers on two screens: certainty, a home anchor, loaded words, asking twice, the setting, borrowed trust.

What struck me most was how well designed it is, as persuasion:

1. **Certainty against fear:** "guaranteed" against "determined by your bank" turns a charge into insurance against a risk that barely exists.
2. **The home-currency anchor:** a flag and a total in pounds feel familiar; the euro option shows no total in pounds, so there is nothing to compare.
3. **Loaded wording:** "without conversion" sounds like going without a service.
4. **Asking twice:** a second chance to catch a tired traveller, and the first answer framed as a mistake to correct.
5. **The setting:** a machine in the street, often with a queue behind you; nobody does the arithmetic there.
6. **Borrowed trust:** it is a bank's machine, so "guaranteed" reads as the bank looking after you.

## What you can do

- **Always choose the local currency:** "without conversion", or "pay in euros", at cash machines and card terminals.
- **Ask for the mark-up:** the law says it must be shown as a percentage over the ECB rate before you confirm.
- **Complain in Portugal** where it is not shown: to the bank, in the Livro de Reclamações (the official complaints book, also online), and to Banco de Portugal through its Portal do Cliente Bancário.
