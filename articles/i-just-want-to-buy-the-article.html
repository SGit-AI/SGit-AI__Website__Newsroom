I just want to buy the article: what happened when I tried to pay Harvard Business Review for one piece I wanted to answer, the arithmetic of a subscription, and ten things HBR could sell a reader like me, most of them already running on this newsroom

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-10-17 · article v1.2.0, 3 versions · newsroom v0.12.92 · newspublishingbusiness-modelsmicropaymentspaywallssubscriptionshbrreading-metergraphslicensingarticle

Abstract: I wanted to answer an article in Harvard Business Review. I read about it on LinkedIn, clicked the link in the comments, and was told that "If you only read HBR on Linkedin, you're only getting part of the story", then offered three subscriptions, from £11 a month to £105 a year. I do not want a subscription. I want to pay for the article, and I would happily pay HBR a fair price for it, because it is worth paying for. The arithmetic says what fair is: HBR.org's own feed lists 45 articles in September 2026, so a month of its digital plan works out at about 24p an article if I read them all, or 55p if I read twenty. What it asks of someone who wants one article is £11, or $11.95 for the article as a reprint, priced as a document for a team. That gap is why people like me do not pay. And the article is the least of what HBR could sell me. The graph of its argument, the vocabulary behind it, the evidence it rests on, a briefing for my board, a licence to quote it in my own work with a share of what my readers pay, and a check that I used it correctly: each of these is a billable unit, and most of them run today on this newsroom, with the reader's data kept by the reader and the code open for any publisher to use.

In short

  • I wanted to pay. HBR is worth paying for, and I wanted to answer one of its articles.
  • I was offered a subscription. £11 a month, or £105 a year, for one article.
  • The arithmetic says 24p to 55p. A month's price, divided by the articles in a month.
  • The reprint is priced for a team. $11.95 a copy, $9.75 each for five to ten.
  • The article is the least of it. The graph, the vocabulary, the evidence, the briefings, the licence to quote.
  • Each of those is a billable unit. Most of them run today on this newsroom.
  • LinkedIn readers are a market, not a leak. Right now the only way to pay is to subscribe.

What happened

On Saturday I saw Olivier Laplace's post on LinkedIn about Turn Vibe-Coded Prototypes Into Tools Your Company Can Actually Use, the Harvard Business Review article he wrote with José Parra Moyano, Karl Schmedders and Benjamin Torben-Nielsen. I wanted to answer it, because I think Simon Wardley's explorers, villagers and town planners explain the problem it describes, and I wrote that answer as Who will fix your mess?.

So I clicked the link in the comments. This is what I got.

"If you only read HBR on Linkedin, you're only getting part of the story." Then "Subscribe now", and three plans.

Digital at £8.75 a month billed yearly, or £11 monthly. Digital & Print at £10, or £13. Premium at £15, or £18. Every one of them is a subscription. None of them is the article.

There is one other door, and I only found it later: a "Buy Copies" link on the article page, which leads to HBR's store. There the article is sold as a reprint for $11.95, or $9.75 a copy if you buy five to ten. That is not a way to buy an article. It is a way to buy a document for a team, priced as one.

I want to be clear about where I stand. HBR is one of the most respected business publications there is. I want to pay for it. I do not want to subscribe to it, because I know how subscriptions end: I pay for months I do not read, and one article ends up costing me a hundred pounds. I wrote about exactly that this week. And I am not alone: a reader who wants one article is a customer, and right now there is no way to be one.

The arithmetic

What would a fair price for one article be? HBR has already told us, if we divide.

HBR.org's own feed of its latest articles lists 45 articles published in September 2026, not counting podcasts, sponsored pieces or the magazine. A month of the digital plan is £11. If I read every one of those 45, I am paying about 24p an article. If I read twenty, about 55p. Even if I read only ten, it is £1.10.

So the price I would expect to pay for one article, read once, is somewhere between 25p and a pound, and I would pay more for a piece I liked. What I am asked is £11, or $11.95. The difference between those two numbers is the whole problem. It is not that readers like me will not pay. It is that the smallest thing we are allowed to buy is between ten and forty-five times bigger than what we want.

How it works here

This newsroom has run the other way since 11 October, with real payments. Every page has a price of a few pence, 10p for a new article and 5p for an older one, and you pay for the share of it you read, after you read it, from a balance kept in your own browser. You never hit a wall: the balance can go below zero and nothing is blocked. At the foot of each article you say how useful it was, and that sets what you pay, from nothing to double. If something was wrong with the page, you say so and you are not charged. It is all on the page for publishers, and the argument is in Pay after you read and Paying after the value.

That is what I wanted on HBR. Read the article, pay for what I read, and say what I thought of it.

The article is the least of what they could sell me

Paying for one article is only the start. Think about who arrives at an HBR article from LinkedIn: very often, someone like me, an executive who wants to use it at work, quote it, follow it up, and take its advice back to a team. Everything that person needs next is something HBR could sell, and each is a billable unit.

My reading. A place that keeps what I read on HBR, how much of each piece I actually read rather than scrolled past, and my highlights, kept by me, not by the publisher. This newsroom's latest work is exactly that: Scrolled is not read separates the two, and the reader's vault keeps it encrypted, under the reader's own keys, across devices. A personalised HBR, as a board member or as a security lead, is a persona, and readers pay to keep a good one because it helps them.

The argument, as a graph. I would like to take an article's argument into my company: its claims, its methods, its examples, connected. The articles here publish their argument as a graph. HBR's would be worth far more, because people would build on it.

The vocabulary. HBR has spent decades naming things in management. Its ontologies and taxonomies, verified by its editors, are a product. That is what fractal semantic graphs are for: each body of knowledge kept in its own vocabulary, connected to others without anyone having to share a schema.

The evidence. How do I check that what an article says is correct? Not a link at the bottom, but the references as a graph I can follow, where each step I take is a page I pay a little for, and newer material costs more than the archive, as it does here. The future of news is the story vault, not the paywall, and a vault of evidence is what it looks like.

Other versions. The short version and the long one. A briefing for my CFO, my CISO, my board. Research, delivered as a vault shows one finding written for six different readers; one article, five readers does the same for this site.

A licence to quote, with a share. This is the one I want most today. I wrote a response to HBR's article. I should be able to quote it properly, use parts of it, and show my readers the passages I am answering. Better still, I should be able to charge my readers a little more for that page and pass it on to HBR, so that my answer makes HBR money instead of competing with it. Nobody offers this yet.

A check of my use. If I quote HBR, or apply its advice, an agent that checks I did it correctly would be worth paying for, to me and to HBR, whose name is on what I quote.

And the next steps. Where is the guidance I can follow, the framework as something my team can run, the follow-up articles? Each of those is another page, and every page is another sale.

The LinkedIn loop

There is a bigger point in that modal. "If you only read HBR on Linkedin, you're only getting part of the story" treats LinkedIn as a leak. I think it is HBR's best market.

The people who read and discuss its articles on LinkedIn are the readers it wants: executives, consultants, founders. Today, the only way any of them can give HBR money is to become a subscriber, which is a tall order, or already be one. Everyone else reads the post, maybe the summary, and moves on. HBR does not learn what they thought, does not get paid by them, and does not get the comments and answers that make an article travel. A post like Olivier's, with fifty reactions and twenty-one comments, is a room full of people HBR has no way to sell to.

The reader was always the product explains how news ended up here, and the agent is the reader explains who is arriving next: agents that will pay for graphs and signed claims, because it is cheaper than not paying.

What I would say to HBR

You have the content, the brand and the readers. What you do not have is a way for most of them to pay you.

  1. Sell me the article. For pennies to a pound, after I read it, with a way to say it was not worth it.
  2. Sell me what is behind it. The graph, the vocabulary, the evidence, the versions.
  3. License my quotes, and share in what they earn. Turn every answer to your articles into revenue.
  4. Let me keep my reading. My record, my highlights, my personas, in a vault I own.
  5. Treat LinkedIn as a storefront. The people discussing your articles there want to pay you.

None of this needs to be invented. It runs today on newsroom.sgit.ai, a small publication with real payments, and the reader's side of it is open source at api.newsroom.sgit.ai, for any publisher to run on its own pages.

What this does not claim

  • HBR's prices are as I saw them. To a UK reader on 17 October 2026; other countries and offers will differ.
  • The 45 is a lower bound. It is HBR.org's own feed for September, without the magazine, podcasts or sponsored pieces.
  • Our numbers are small. This newsroom has few readers; it shows the model works, not that it scales.
  • Two of the ten units are proposals. The licence to quote with a share, and the check of my use, are not built here.
  • I have a stake. I build this newsroom and its reader code, and I would like publishers to use it.

If you work at HBR, or at any publisher thinking about this, write to agent@riskmandate.ai.
