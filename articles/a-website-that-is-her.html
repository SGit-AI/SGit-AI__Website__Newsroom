A website that is her: how Yiheng Wang's site was made in three passes in one evening, by the site factory, five design agents and then her own agent, with five designs, a kitchen brigade of 26 agents, a printable brief and a page for your AI, and why every claim on it is something she can talk about because she did it

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-11-16 · article v1.0.0 · newsroom v0.14.129 · careersagenticcellssite-factorydesignagentsweb-summitarticle

Abstract: This is the full circle I have been working towards. Yiheng Wang, who ran one of the largest agent teams at Web Summit with no technical background, is looking for a role as a project or operations manager, and tonight she got a website that is genuinely her. It was made in three passes, in one evening. First, the AgentiCCells site factory: a request with a brief and pointers to her public work, and a CI pipeline created the repository, the DNS entry, GitHub Pages and HTTPS in 72 seconds, with Claude Code's first version pushed three and a half minutes after the request. Then a completely separate session, with the site's own instructions, read-write access and more of her photos and sources, which called in five design agents: in about eleven minutes of commits it built five designs of the same facts, The Profile, Mission Control, The Pass, The Brief and The Journey, with her 26 agents drawn as a constellation, a live network and a kitchen brigade, a prep list, a ticket, a five-course menu, an incident log, a recruiter's page that prints, and a page that tells a hiring manager how to ask their own AI about her. Then her own agent took over, working for her. None of it is made up. Every number names its public source, and every one is something she can talk about in an interview, because she did it. That is the power, and it is only possible because of everything around it: agents that have read our guidance, a factory, behaviour policies, and public, linkable work.

In short

  • A website that is her: yiheng-wang.agenticcells.site, for Yiheng Wang, who is looking for a project or operations manager role.
  • Three passes, one evening: the site factory, five design agents, then her own agent.
  • Five designs of the same facts, each telling her story a different way, with a picker.
  • Her 26 agents drawn three ways: a live network, a constellation and a kitchen brigade.
  • Made to hire her: a printable brief, interview questions, and a page that tells your AI how to ask about her.
  • Nothing made up: every claim names its source, and every claim is something she did and can talk about.
  • The full circle: it works because of everything we built around it.

Who she is

Yiheng Wang (the sources from the summit call her Vicky) was the project manager for the RiskMandate team at Web Summit, one of the three editors of PrompTugal.news, and ran one of the largest agent teams we had, mostly through one conductor agent, with no technical background (don't micromanage your agents). Her agents made 1,171 commits to PrompTugal.news and her journalists wrote about 53 of its 73 articles. Before that she was a pastry chef, then in product development, then project management. In her own words, from her approved interview: "In a kitchen I managed recipes, standards and a team. Now I manage agents and rules. It's the same job underneath: set the standard, hand out the work, and check the result."

She is now looking for a role as a project manager or operations manager, and she agreed to have this site made and to be named. This is exactly what I wrote this afternoon that everyone looking for a job should have: a site that shows the evidence (show me the evidence). A few hours later, she had one.

Three passes, one evening

Pass one: the factory. I gave the cells agent a request: her name, the roles she wants, and where to look for her work at the summit. It committed a brief, and the AgentiCCells site factory did the rest in CI (the site factory works): the repository at 18:33:37, the DNS record in Route 53 visible in public DNS at 18:33:56, the custom domain, the deploy, and HTTPS served at 18:34:44, 72 seconds after the request was validated. Claude Code's first version, written from the brief and her public sources, was pushed at 18:37:02. That version was clean and correct, and plain.

Pass two: five design agents. She prefers Yiheng to Vicky, so the site moved to her name. Then, in a completely separate Claude session, I followed the instructions on the site itself, with read-write access, gave it more of her photos and a couple more references to what she had done, and asked for something much better: five different designs, focused on hiring her, with her photo throughout. It built a shared base (photos, her agents as data, a design picker, a question bank) and then five design agents, each with one design. The commits for the five designs ran from 19:56 to 20:07.

Pass three: her own agent. Then the site was handed to her. Since 20:44, the commits are by site@AgentiCCells.yiheng, an agent working for her: her new photos, her contact buttons, her words in "In one minute", The Pass as the home page, and a pass to cut the labels so each fact is said once. She runs her site the way she ran her agents.

Five designs, the same facts

The site has one master copy of the facts, as Markdown, and five designs that tell it differently. A picker at the top lets you choose; your choice stays in your browser.

  • The Profile: warm and editorial, like a magazine profile, with her name set huge and a photo from the stand.
  • Mission Control: dark and live: her agents as a working network, her decisions as an incident log, her lessons as numbered rules.
  • The Pass: the pastry kitchen and the agent team as the same brigade, from the prep list to the reservation card.
  • The Brief: for recruiters, one clear page that prints, with competencies mapped to evidence and questions to ask her.
  • The Journey: photo-led, her path in eight numbered chapters, from the kitchen to the storm night to the newsroom.

Her agents, drawn three ways

The same data, her 26 agents and their commits from her dashboard, is drawn three ways. Mission Control shows it as a live network you can point at, each agent with its commit count. The Profile draws a constellation: her at the centre, one conductor, four groups, each dot sized by its commits. The Pass draws it as a kitchen: Yiheng as head of the pass, the conductor that calls the orders, the reviewer and checker at the pass ("nothing goes out unchecked"), the newsroom desks as stations, operations as back of house, and the specialists and safeguards keeping it safe.

That last one is my favourite, because it is not decoration. It is her own analogy, from her own interview, turned into a diagram that explains how an agent team works better than most I have seen.

The kitchen, all the way through

The Pass carries the analogy all the way. Her set-up before the summit is a prep list, with the dates she reached each level. Her spot check is ticket 027: the reviewer reported 15 articles fixed, she opened some herself, found two it had missed and sent it back, and the second pass found 12 more, 27 in all. Her path is a menu in five courses, from pastry chef to "your project or operations team". And the call to action is a reservation card: book Yiheng for your team.

Decisions, rules and chapters

Mission Control turns her decisions into an incident log, each with what happened, what she decided and the outcome: the storm night, the image check, the conductor hand-over, the stop files, whether to sign tasks. Her lessons become rules, quoted from her interview: start with one conductor and only talk to that one; when an agent says it's done, check a few things yourself; don't add a rule as soon as you think of it.

The Journey tells the same facts as a story: chapter one, the kitchen; chapter three, two weeks from zero, with her setup levels climbing to L6 on 3 November; chapter five, the storm night, when Web Summit called off a day and she set up a temporary agent and changed the home page; chapter six, the newsroom, with about 53 of 73 articles drawn as squares and 15 + 12 = 27 as a bar.

Made to hire her

The Brief is for recruiters: a candidate profile in one minute, six key results each with its source, competencies mapped to evidence ("what she did, where it is written down, no self-ratings"), her experience, and five questions to ask her in an interview, each from a story in the sources. How did you decide when to stop checking? What did you do in the first hour when a researcher kept running after a stop?

And the page I think is the future: "Use this site with AI". A hiring manager pastes one line into ChatGPT or Claude, with the role they are hiring for, and their assistant reads the whole site and its sources and answers with a link for each point, saying plainly where the site does not say something. Yiheng gets her own instructions, to change the site the way she ran her agents. Her own AI gets a question bank, to interview her one question at a time for the facts that are still in her head. And an editor, me for example, gets instructions to add materials, with her having the last word.

It is her

This is the point I most want to make. None of this is made up. Every figure on every design carries a marker that links to its public source and the date it was read: her approved interview, the team's pages, her dashboard, the newsroom's articles. There are no invented employers, dates or testimonials; where something is missing, the site says so ("contact details: Yiheng will add these here herself").

So every one of these things is something she can talk about, because she did it. A hiring manager can ask about the storm night, the 27 fixes, the conductor hand-over or the stop files, and she was there. That is what I always tell people: do the work in public, so it can be linked, and then the evidence speaks for you (act as the job you want). Here, the evidence was so good that the agents could make something spectacular from it, without inventing a word.

The full circle

This is a full circle moment for me, and it is worth saying why it worked.

  • Agents that have learned our way of working. Not trained in the traditional sense, but they have read the guidance, the markdowns, the briefs and the reality files, and created material with them. So a new session could pick up the site's own instructions and go.
  • The factory. A commit becomes a repository, a DNS entry and a live site in a minute, so the agents' time goes on the site, not the plumbing.
  • Behaviour policies. Each agent knows what it may touch, which is what frees it to be creative within it (the cell is the unit).
  • Public, linkable work. Ten days of it, from her and her agents, at the summit (ten days at Web Summit).
  • A handover. The site ends where it should, with her own agent, working for her.

And it is a template now. Everything I described this afternoon for the job-seeker cell exists here, built by agents in an evening, for a real person, from real evidence. Fork it.

What this does not claim

  • The site keeps changing: it is hers now, and her agent edits it; the screenshots are from the evening of 16 November.
  • The timings are from the factory's status file and the repository's commits; the design time is the span of the design commits, not the whole session.
  • The figures on the site are its own, each with its source; the agent counts come from her dashboard and differ slightly from other counts, as the site itself explains.
  • She has the last word on everything about her.
