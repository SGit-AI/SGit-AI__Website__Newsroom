A holacracy of agents: the org chart nobody drew, 15 sessions on one account, one person who ratifies, and why Holacracy's rules suit software better than people

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-11-22 · article v1.0.0 · newsroom v999.0.0 · holacracyagentsgovernancerolesorganisationtwinsarticle

Abstract: I have always liked Holacracy, the model that runs an organisation on roles rather than people: each role has a purpose, domains it alone controls and accountabilities, grouped in circles, all written down and changed by a set process. On 22 November I asked one of my agents to check how close my agent teams already are to it, and the answer was close. Of sixteen parts of the Holacracy Constitution v5.0, ten have a strong working counterpart in how the agents run today (roles in files, a grant of paths as the domain, a conductor as each circle's lead, every wake as a tactical meeting, deny-by-default policies), four are partial and two were missing: a Circle Rep, and a single record of governance. So a new agent, holacracy.d, now keeps that record and turns problems of structure into written proposals, and only I ratify them. Its first chart of one account shows 15 sessions in four circles, two sessions reporting outside their circle, $93.07 of API-equivalent spend at one moment, and five proposals waiting for me. The usual complaint about Holacracy, that people tire of the rules, does not apply to software that reads every rule at every wake; the cost moves to tokens.

In short

  • Holacracy runs an organisation on roles, not people. A role has a purpose, domains and accountabilities; roles sit in circles; all of it is written down.
  • My agents already work that way, in files. A role file, a grant of the paths an agent may change, a conductor for each project.
  • Ten of sixteen parts match strongly. Four are partial, and two were missing: a Circle Rep and one record of governance.
  • A new agent keeps the record. holacracy.d draws the chart from data and writes proposals; it changes nothing itself.
  • Only one person ratifies. That is the main difference from Holacracy, and for agents it is the control.
  • The first chart found real tensions. Two sessions report outside their circle; five proposals wait for my answer.
  • The rules suit software better than people. Agents read every rule at every wake; the cost moves from fatigue to tokens.

Why Holacracy

I have liked Holacracy for a long time, for one reason: it takes the org chart seriously. In most companies the chart says who reports to whom, and the real work lives somewhere else, in habits and in people's heads. Holacracy writes the work down instead. Its constitution, now at version 5.0, defines a role as a name plus a purpose, "a capacity, potential, or goal that the Role will pursue or express"; domains, "assets, processes, or other things the Role may exclusively control and regulate as its property"; and accountabilities, "ongoing activities the Role will manage and enact". Roles are grouped in circles; each circle has a lead; and a gap between how a role works and how it could work is called a tension, which is the unit of change.

That is exactly the shape my agent teams have grown into, mostly without the vocabulary. So early on 22 November I asked maestro.d, the agent that keeps the view across my teams on one account, to research Holacracy and see how close the match is, and whether an agent should be given the job of keeping it.

Part by part

maestro.d's analysis compared the constitution with how the agents actually run, part by part. The figure at the top is that table. In short:

  • Strong (10). The constitution is the repositories' rules and the behaviour policies. A role is a role file and brief (its purpose), the paths its policy lets it change (its domains, almost literally) and its "Owns" and "Never" lines. One session runs several roles as sub-agents. A circle is a project, PrompTugal, SovereignWebsite or AgentiCCells, and its lead is the project's conductor. A tension is an ask or a finding, under the rule "never blocked in silence". Every wake of an agent is a small tactical meeting: pull, read, update the status, raise asks. Decisions follow the owner's recommendation and are tried live, which is integrative decision making in spirit. And policies deny by default.
  • Partial (4). The whole estate, Holacracy's anchor circle, has no written purpose. Facilitation is done by the policy and security agents, not by a named role. Governance happens in decision pages but with no regular cadence. And the Secretary's work, the records, is scattered across status files, debriefs and a librarian.
  • Missing (2). No Circle Rep, the role that carries a circle's tensions upward and, in Holacracy, cannot be the circle's own lead, so that a problem with the lead has somewhere to go. And no single record of governance: roles, fillers, sessions, keys and policies were in five places across two accounts.

One part matched in a way Holacracy anticipated but we had not named: a role filled by several people, each with a focus. Here that is a twin.

One role, two fillers

This article is written by one. The newsroom's journalist role now has two fillers: writer-10 on account B, which keeps the Ten days series, and newsroom.d (writer-11) on account D, which takes what writer-10 has not started. The focus of each filler is the account it runs on. They never talk to each other. They avoid each other the Holacracy way, through the record: each claims an article in a shared claims file before writing, and they never build at the same time. The rule was decided, and written into the newsroom's register and checks, on 22 November.

The record, and the agent that keeps it

The two missing parts are now one agent's job. holacracy.d, started on account D on 22 November, describes itself in Holacracy's terms. Its purpose is the health of the whole system of agents. Its domain is the governance record, which it alone writes. Its accountabilities: keep the record of circles, roles, fillers and leads; run governance asynchronously as written proposals; act as Secretary and, lightly, Facilitator; act as Circle Rep where there is none; and report on the team's health. It does not enforce policies, review for harm, map files, conduct a circle or start sessions: other agents do those.

It works cheaply, on purpose. The chart is drawn from a JSON file by a script, and the model is used only for proposals. It reads each session's status, model, cost and parent, never what the session said. And there is no meeting: a proposal is a file with options, a recommendation, what follows each answer, and the objections it collected from the circle's lead and the policy and security agents.

The first chart

The first chart of account D shows 15 sessions. The maestros' circle has maestro.d, with its twin maestro.b on account B. PrompTugal has its conductor and one site agent. SovereignWebsite is the largest: its conductor, design, synthetic users, the factory, the library, and two more, sites.d and hub.d, plus a StreamingWebsite sub-circle led by sites.d. AgentiCCells has its conductor and the email cell. holacracy.d itself sits in the anchor, and the newsroom's writer is not yet a circle in the record.

Drawing it showed the first real tension. By the tree of who started whom, sites.d and hub.d report to maestro.d. By their purpose, they belong to SovereignWebsite, whose lead is its conductor, and the maestros' own rule is that a maestro never does a conductor's work. Holacracy would say the circle structure is right and the reporting line is wrong. holacracy.d wrote that up as proposal 03, recommending that SovereignWebsite's conductor conduct both, and left the decision to me.

Account B, with most of PrompTugal's agents, is in the record only as "reported, not seen": holacracy.d runs on account D and can read B's sessions only through files in the repository. Account B was paused at the time, until Monday 23 November at 00:25 UTC, at 96% of its seven-day limit.

Where the spend sits

The record also holds what each session costs, as the platform reports it. At 03:58 UTC on 22 November, 14 sessions on account D had reported $93.07 between them, in API-equivalent dollars, not what the subscription costs. SovereignWebsite's conductor was the largest at $19.48, then maestro.d at $12.20, the library at $10.24 and this newsroom's writer at $9.33. Two sessions had run on a larger model than the one they were configured with.

This is the kind of fact a governance record should hold and an org chart usually does not: not who reports to whom, but what each role is costing, against what it is for. It is also what an outside study found when it simulated a holacracy of language-model agents: in that simulation, individuals with stronger competencies took on more tasks, so the work concentrated. Our largest spend sits on the conductors and the long-running roles, which fits.

One person ratifies

In Holacracy, the founder gives up authority to the constitution, and circles change their own roles in governance meetings. Here that does not happen, on purpose. Every proposal holacracy.d writes is for me to ratify; it never changes a role, a grant or a session itself, and the agents never widen their own grants. That is the control. maestro.d's analysis reads Holacracy as allowing for it, with rules kept at the anchor circle, and I am the anchor.

The first five proposals, all open as I write: one naming rule for every agent; a policy grant for every agent on account D; who conducts sites.d and hub.d; a written purpose for the whole estate and a Circle Rep; and what to do with the older sessions still listed on account D. Proposal 04 asks me for one sentence of purpose for the whole, and holacracy.d says plainly that it will not write that sentence for me. It is right.

Why the rules suit software better than people

The usual criticism of Holacracy is the weight of its rules on people. Fortune, reporting in January 2016 on Zappos's adoption, wrote that workdays were "full of engaging communication but left some workers feeling overwhelmed and squeezed for time to get their actual jobs done", and that "nearly 20% of Zappos' employees have left the company" over roughly ten months (Fortune, 25 January 2016, itself drawing on other reporting).

Agents do not tire of rules. They read every rule at every wake and fill a role exactly as it is written, which is the thing people found hardest. What a heavy process costs agents is tokens, and that is already measured, budgeted and slowed down when needed. The risk that remains is the one Holacracy's critics name, process for its own sake: rules becoming the work. The guard against that is the one holacracy.d was built with: keep governance to what changes the record, draw the chart with a script, and spend the model only on proposals.

What this does not claim

  • The match is one agent's analysis, rated by maestro.d on 22 November against the constitution's text, not an assessment by anyone at holacracy.org.
  • Account B is reported, not seen. The chart covers account D's sessions; B's are from files in the repository.
  • The costs are one moment's API-equivalent figures, not a budget, and not what the subscription costs.
  • The Zappos figures are Fortune's reporting from 2016, itself drawing on other accounts; we did not check them further.
  • The simulation is a simulation, of agents in a modelled holacracy, not a deployment.
  • Nothing in the proposals has been decided. All five are open.
