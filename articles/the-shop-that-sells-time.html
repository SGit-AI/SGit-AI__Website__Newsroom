The shop that sells time: the padel shop's path to market, sell one and fulfil it before anything else, start with the customers who already trust you, promise late and deliver early, a few products you vouch for, and no stock until the orders make you

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-11-03 · article v1.0.0 · newsroom v0.14.50 · ecommercesmall-businesspadelpath-to-marketstocktrusthostingagentsarticle

Abstract: In the first article about the padel shop, we mapped what a coach in the Algarve needs to sell a sleeve: the law, the platforms, the mock-up. This one is about the path to market, and the advice I would give anyone building an early shop. It is very easy to start too big, with grand claims and a site that looks like a company. The only questions that matter at the start are: can you make a sale, and can you fulfil it? What you are really selling is time, knowledge and trust: the moment you take something off a person's to-do list, you have solved their problem, whether it arrives tomorrow or in a week. So the shop should be personal, run on best effort, and start with the customers who already trust you, because if you cannot sell to them, you will not sell to anyone else. Promise late and deliver early, because people are upset by expectations that are not met, not by waiting. Sell a few products you use and vouch for, and experiment around them. And above all, hold no stock until the orders force you to: the numbers show why. The first months are a university. Keep the site simple and cheap, on a host that allows a shop, and let agents do the rest.

In short

  • Can you make a sale, and can you fulfil it? Nothing else matters at the start.
  • You sell time, knowledge and trust. An offer takes the problem off the buyer's list.
  • Personal and best effort, starting with the customers who already trust you.
  • Promise late, deliver early. People are upset by broken expectations, not by waiting.
  • A few products you vouch for, and experiments around them.
  • No stock until the orders force you to; the numbers show why.
  • The first months are a university. Scale when fulfilment takes an hour or two a day.
  • Keep the site simple and cheap, on a host that allows a shop.

Sell one, and fulfil it

When people build an early shop, or any early service, it is very easy to go over the top: a lot of products, grand claims, a site that tries to look much bigger than the business behind it. None of that answers the two questions that matter: can you make a sale, and can you fulfil it?

At the start it does not have to be efficient. It just has to work. Paul Graham said it in 2013, in "Do things that don't scale": "Some startups could be entirely manual at first. If you can find someone with a problem that needs solving and you can solve it manually, go ahead and do that for as long as you can, and then gradually automate the bottlenecks."

What you are selling

The sleeve is the product. What you are selling is time, knowledge and trust.

Here is how it went for me. On day one, after a lesson, I was told I should buy a sleeve for my arm. That was the problem handed to me, as I wrote in a solution, not a problem. On day two I still did not have it. Day three, the same. On day five I had another lesson with the same coach, and still no sleeve. Another week and I would be back in England, where I could probably order it, so even if I buy it now it will arrive about then. Today I am going to a pharmacy to buy something a padel teacher recommended.

You might think two or three days is a long wait. It is not. The point is to take the problem off my to-do list. While it is on the list, it uses my attention, it is one more thing to remember, one more context I hold. If someone says "I can get it for you; it arrives in five days", it is gone, whether it takes two days or ten. That is what I would have paid for.

Personal, and best effort

Which is why the shop should be personal. In the UK I started hearing about EverythingPadel. As I was told it, one person who made videos and ran a small shop, who replied to every email and gave really good advice: you should buy your racket from him. Its own page today says it was "Founded by Ewan Ramsden", and its review profile that it "started from a small shed". Its YouTube channel, which joined YouTube in January 2021, has 136,000 subscribers. The reply to every email is my memory of what people told me, not something I could check; the connection with one person is the point either way.

A personal shop gets a margin of error a company does not. People understand that it is one person, they want to help, and a late parcel from someone they know is a different thing from a late parcel from a brand. Graham again: "It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user."

Start with the customers who trust you

If you are a professional, your first market is your current customers. Do not go much beyond it. If you cannot serve the market you have today, you will not serve any other.

Take the padel coach: if he cannot sell me the sleeve he recommended, something is wrong, because the trust is already there and it is not working. Every lesson he gives is a sale he is not making.

Promise late, deliver early

In early retail, always under-promise and over-deliver. Say it will take five days, and deliver in two. You are on the safe side, and you never upset an expectation. People are rarely upset because something did not happen; they are upset because what happened did not match what they expected. Say "tomorrow", and they make plans for tomorrow.

That is not only intuition. Richard Oliver's model of 1980, cited thousands of times since, "expresses consumer satisfaction as a function of expectation and expectancy disconfirmation": satisfaction depends on the gap between what you expected and what you got. I did not find a study that tests early delivery directly, so take the five days and two days as my advice, not a measured result.

And whether you have it in stock is not my concern. I care that I get it, and when. Tell me a day, a week or a month, and I can decide.

A few products you vouch for

Have a small number of products you use and can vouch for: these are the ones I think are right, and here is why. Then experiment around them. Every product on the site that does not sell is feedback. Every one that sells tells you something too: where people hesitate, what they ask, how they use it.

No stock

This is probably my biggest recommendation. For the first months, until you have a decent amount of revenue, trade a bit more friction and a bit more delay for having no stock. Once you hold stock, the whole economics change.

The numbers are not complicated, but they do not lie. Take the sleeve: bought per order at €32.95 and sold at €39.90, the shop makes €6.95 a sale and is never in debt. Now buy fifty up front, at an assumed €25 each to get a better price: €1,250 out on day one. Each sale now makes more, but after a first month of ten sales you are €851 down with forty sleeves in a cupboard, and stock only pulls ahead of buying per order after thirty-eight sales. A Queensland government guide puts it plainly: "Stock on hand is money tied up (asset) that you can't access until the stock is sold. This can impact on cash flow."

And money is only part of it. With stock you have to store it, it can be damaged, and you have made a bet that you picked the right products. You have also set an expectation: if you have it, people will expect it tomorrow.

The first months are a university

For the first three to six months, think of it as going to university. You are learning about shipping, customer service, fulfilment, returns, payments, and a large number of other things you cannot learn any other way. Sell one, sell five, sell ten. It is much harder than you think.

Having far too many orders is a lovely problem, and it is the moment to scale, not before. My rule of thumb: when fulfilling orders takes an hour or two a day, go to the next level.

The same was true of businesses that are now large. Photobox, founded in 2000, was described by its co-founder Graham Hobson as "basically a website, order management system and a big fat printer". The story I remember is that in the early days the founders printed the photos themselves and posted them; I could not find a source I could read that confirms it. Everyone could print their own photos, but they did not: they wanted it done, and they wanted someone who knew how. Photobox Group went on to buy Moonpig in 2011.

Keep it simple, and on a host that allows a shop

The site should be simple, cheap and easy to change, because you do not yet know where the orders will come from. You could even start on email. When you do build it, there is one thing to check that I did not check before.

GitHub Pages is free, and it is where this newsroom lives, but GitHub's terms say it is "not intended for or allowed to be used as a free web hosting service to run your online business, e-commerce site, or any other website that is primarily directed at either facilitating commercial transactions". So a shop should not be on it, and that corrects the setup in the link the coach should have sent, which suggested GitHub Pages or Shopify. Vercel's free plan is "restricted to non-commercial personal use only". Netlify's free plan says: "you can deploy commercial projects", with a monthly allowance that, if exceeded, suspends the site until the next month. Cloudflare Pages serves static files "free and unlimited" on its free plan, and I found no rule against a shop. Either keeps the code in a GitHub repository, where agents can work on it, and publishes from there.

The setup around it

This is where the work we do helps. The shop's source in a repository an agent maintains; the customer list as a CRM in an encrypted vault, never on the public site; drafts of every email written by an agent and sent by the person; a dedicated Google Workspace and a Claude account for the business, as I described for a teacher with agents she can trust. That is a full company setup, run by one person, and it makes experiments cheap: a new product page is an afternoon, and taking it down is a minute.

What this does not claim

  • The stock numbers are illustrative; the €25 bulk price is assumed, not quoted by a supplier.
  • EverythingPadel's reply to every email, and Photobox's founders printing at home, are what I remember being told; neither could be confirmed from a source I could read.
  • No study tests early delivery directly; Oliver's model is about expectations in general.
  • Hosting terms change; they were read on 3 November 2026, and Cloudflare's position on shops comes from the absence of a rule, not a statement.
  • My own story is told as it happened, with the details of the arm left out.
