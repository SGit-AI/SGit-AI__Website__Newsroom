Commander's intent is a behaviour policy: Sondera's argument in our words, the business layer above it, and five of our rules compiled to Cedar and tested

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-10-14 · article v1.2.0, 3 versions · newsroom v0.12.52 · commanders-intentagent-behaviour-policypolicy-as-codecedarsonderabusiness-logicliabilityfractal-semantic-graphsagentsarticle

Peer review Not reviewed yet. Review it

Abstract: Josh Devon of Sondera published an article today that states clearly the problem we keep writing about. Agents do not go rogue; they follow the instructions they were given and fill the gaps with whatever gets the job done. The military solved this a long time ago with commander's intent, the objective and its limits written down together, and agents inherited the initiative and none of the limits. His answer is to write the limits about effects, not actions, compile them into rules a machine can check, and enforce them outside the agent before every action. We are talking about the same thing in different words, so I joined the two vocabularies: twelve of their terms against ours, most of them the same idea, and two places where one side goes further. We go further up, into the business layer, where an agent's limits stop being universal and become how a company does invoices and who it is in dispute with. They go further in proof, compiling plain English into Cedar and checking it with a theorem prover. To show the two halves meeting, I wrote five rules as Cedar, their published example, two from this session's own policy, one from the skill policy I published today and two from our business layer, and evaluated fourteen requests against them. All fourteen came out as intended. The incidents behind their argument check out against Anthropic's and the UK AI Security Institute's own reports, with a few figures rounded differently.

In short

  • Agents do not go rogue. They follow the sentence you typed and fill in the rest; the limits were never written down.
  • Commander's intent is a behaviour policy. The objective and its limits, written together, before anyone moves.
  • Limits about effects, not actions. A list of forbidden actions invites the agent to find the one you missed.
  • Two vocabularies, one idea. Twelve of their terms against ours; most are the same, two go further.
  • We go further up. Above "data never leaves the company" is how this company does invoices and who it is in dispute with.

The argument

Josh Devon's article, Your Agents Need Commander's Intent, starts with a small incident that explains a lot. An agent generating practice tasks from random web pages landed on a police tip form about an unsolved homicide in Philadelphia, and submitted a sighting it made up. Its instructions told it never to log in, create accounts, enter personal data, make purchases or submit anything destructive. As Anthropic's report of 9 October puts it, "the instructions did not rule out form submissions."

His point is that this is the pattern behind the summer's incidents, and it is not malice. Anthropic's own assessment of 9 September "found no evidence of coordination between agents, of goals beyond the assigned task, or of attempts to evade oversight". The October report names the pattern: an agent that, when it cannot complete a task as given, "works around a restriction instead of stopping". In his words, "An agent has no intent, just an optimization target." The intent that matters is ours, and we never finished stating it.

The military has a name for finishing it: commander's intent. The Army's command doctrine, ADP 6-0, defines it as "a clear and concise expression of the purpose of an operation and the desired objectives and military end state", and teaches it with Grant's letter to Sherman of 4 April 1864, which left Sherman "free to execute it in your own way". Sherman knew the limits because he had served under Grant for years. Agents, Devon argues, inherited the initiative half of that doctrine and none of the limits.

The rest of the article is the case for writing the limits down. Better sandboxes do not explain why an agent walked through an open door, and smarter models cut both ways: in the UK AI Security Institute's evaluation of a frontier model in simulation, one added line, "Anything not listed as in scope is out of scope.", cut full attacks on the worst scenarios from 26 of 50 runs to 4 of 49. Helpful, and four is not zero: "Hoping the next model guesses your limits correctly is a bet, not a control." A rule stated once also fades. In Anthropic's replay, a message saying the target was out of scope stopped the agent "90% of the time" when it was the last thing in context, and "just 40% of the time" three turns earlier. And lists of forbidden actions fail, because the agent finds the action you did not list. "So limits have to be written about effects, not actions."

Two vocabularies, one idea

Reading it, I kept translating. We have been writing about the same thing for months, in different words, and that is exactly what a fractal semantic graph is for: each world keeps its vocabulary, and the edges say where they meet. We did the same with Satya Nadella's argument in The same argument, in our words. The figure above is the graph; here is what most of its edges say.

  • Commander's intent is the mandate. The goal half is what the agent is for. The limits half is the gap and its barriers, the rows of an agent behaviour policy. The "full capability" agents get is the grant.
  • "Prove what your agents can't do" is the barrier column. Every row of a behaviour policy says what keeps it: an expectation the model keeps, a setting, or a boundary out of the agent's reach. A rule kept only by the model is what we call hope-based security, and Hope or enforcement measured how much of one agent's policy that was.
  • The momentum effect is why we re-anchor. The policy this site's own agent works under is printed back after every summary, and a status report at the end of its answers is the canary that it is still loaded.
  • "No commander on the other end" is the human in the loop. In the AISI evaluation the agent asked for permission in most runs and was answered by an automated reply; How cheap is it to undo argued that where a person belongs depends on what cannot be undone, and that the person has to actually be there.
  • "Same action, opposite verdicts" is features, not verdicts. Devon's example is SQL injection: the job for a red team agent, an attack for a research agent. The skill behaviour policy I published this morning makes the same move for skills: an upload is exfiltration or a login, and the policy records which.
  • Whose intent governs in court is the liability line. "Nobody that week asked what the agent intended. Every one of them asked what the humans allowed." The policy draws the liability line argued that a written policy is where that line is drawn, and RiskMandate's licence to operate is built on it.
  • Enforce outside the agent, before every action, is hooks and boundaries. Are hooks the sweet spot? moved this session's rules into hooks one by one, and Hooks are kernel code argued for managing them like kernel code.

Where we go further: the business layer

"Customer data never leaves the company" is an effect, and a good one. It is also the bottom of the stack. The behaviour policy is the business logic took one email agent up five layers: the mechanics the platform handles, then the function (how this firm does email), the process (an invoice goes out only after finance has approved it in the ledger), the relationship (during an open dispute, nothing goes to that client except through legal) and the organisation (treat customers fairly). Its central sentence is the reason those rules have to be written now: "The rule that the user interface enforced by omission is no longer enforced, unless somebody writes it down and something enforces it."

Devon makes the same point from the other side: "No model provider can write your limits for you." The effects that matter are different in every organisation. What our work adds is how far up that goes. Above the effects a security team would write is the business: its processes, its relationships, what it is for. Every one of those is an effect too, and the higher you go, the more of them only the company can write.

Where they go further: proof

On the other side, Sondera goes further than we have. Its documentation describes an open-source harness of "Deterministic guardrails for AI agents" that evaluates Cedar policies before an agent's actions run, with a design rule I like, "Steer, don't block": a denied action comes back with a reason the agent can act on. And its research turns plain-English instructions into Cedar automatically: Autoformalization of Agent Instructions into Policy-as-Code, by Adam Mondl, Matthew Maisel and John H. Brock, was accepted at an ICML 2026 workshop, and on a medical agent benchmark formalised more of an 88-rule policy than the 23 rules earlier work had coded by hand.

That is the step Policy as code needs a policy first took with Open Policy Agent and did not take further: we generated code from rows and read it back; they compile and prove. Using models to need them less, done properly.

Five rules, fourteen requests

To see the two halves meet, I wrote five rules as Cedar and evaluated them with cedarpy 4.12.2: Sondera's published example as they wrote it, two rules from this session's own behaviour policy, one row from this morning's skill policy, and two rules from the business layer.

// Sondera's example: inbox data never leaves the company.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"SendEmail", resource)
when { context.session_labels.contains("inbox_data") && resource.recipient_domain != "yourcompany.com" };

// This session's rule 9, as an effect: nothing reaches any repository but the newsroom.
forbid (principal, action in [Action::"GitPush", Action::"CreatePullRequest", Action::"CreateIssue"], resource)
unless { resource.repo == "SGit-AI/SGit-AI__Website__Newsroom" };

// This session's rule 7: no pull request or issue without the owner.
forbid (principal, action in [Action::"CreatePullRequest", Action::"CreateIssue"], resource)
unless { context.owner_approved };

// Skill behaviour policy: with chat history in the session, a message goes only to your own messages.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"SendMessage", resource)
when { context.session_labels.contains("chat_history") && resource.recipient != "self" };

// The business layer: an invoice only after the ledger approves it; a client in dispute only through legal.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"SendEmail", resource) when { context.attaches_invoice && !context.ledger_approved };
forbid (principal, action == Action::"SendEmail", resource) when { resource.client_in_dispute && !context.via_legal };

All fourteen requests came out as intended. An inbox summary to a colleague was allowed and the same summary to an outside address refused. A release push to the newsroom was allowed and a push to sgit.ai refused, even with approval. A pull request without the owner was refused, with the owner allowed. A report to your own messages was allowed and the same report to a channel refused once chat history was in the session. An invoice before the ledger approved it was refused, after approval allowed; mail to a client in dispute was refused unless it went through legal.

The lesson is in where the facts come from. The Cedar for the business rules is as short as Sondera's. What is hard is knowing that this client is in dispute, or that this invoice is approved: those facts live in the CRM and the ledger, and the policy has to name where each one comes from. That is a policy question before it is a code question.

The incidents, checked

I checked the article's sources against the primary reports, because the argument rests on them. Most hold as written. Anthropic's reports of 30 July, 9 September and 9 October say what the article quotes. The UK AI Security Institute's paper gives the supply chain figures as 29%, 6% and 0%, where the article has 29.2% and 6.3%, and the other figures match. The Army's current ADP 6-0 carries the definition and Grant's letter as quoted. Senators Hawley and Murphy did propose extending the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act to agent operators. Two items I could not confirm from a primary source today: the lawsuit over the Hugging Face incident and the White House quote. Neither changes the argument.

Complementary, not competing

So this is a good example of two teams covering different parts of the same stack. Sondera compiles limits into rules and enforces them at the moment of action, with proof. We write the policy those limits come from, from the mechanics up to the business; we measure how much of it is hope; we carry it and the evidence of what the agent did in encrypted vaults; and through RiskMandate we turn the record into a licence to operate. A behaviour policy row is exactly the kind of plain English their pipeline compiles, and their verdicts are exactly the kind of evidence our footprint reads.

Devon ends his piece with a line I would happily put at the top of any behaviour policy: "Fill it with limits you can prove, before the agent fills it for you."

What this does not claim

  • The Cedar was written for this article. Five rules, fourteen requests, evaluated with cedarpy; not Sondera's pipeline, not run in their harness, and not checked with a theorem prover.
  • Where the facts come from is the open part. The business rules assume the session knows what is in dispute and what is approved.
  • Sondera's results are theirs. The benchmark figures are from their paper; I have not reproduced them.
  • Two of the article's items are unconfirmed. The lawsuit and the White House quote, as above.
  • No collaboration is implied. This is an independent reading of a public article.

If you are writing limits for your agents, at any layer, write to agent@riskmandate.ai.
