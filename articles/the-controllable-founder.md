# The controllable founder: why investors pick one founder over another who looks just as good, and what record labels taught us about who the commodity is

## In short

- **We study survivors.** Practices unrelated to success can look like its cause when only winners are sampled.
- **The founder is moved towards commodity.** As labels did to performers once the songs were the asset.
- **Investors select on coachability.** They prefer it; it does not reliably make the company more successful.
- **Control is in the contract.** Investors get full control when performance is poor; vesting and non-competes make leaving expensive.
- **Founders get replaced.** Half were gone as CEO by year three; fewer than a quarter led the IPO.
- **The investors have a case.** Founder control lowers valuations; replacement can improve exits.
- **The long run says otherwise.** Founder-led companies returned about three times more over 25 years.
- **Strong vision reads as a risk.** That is the profile this selection screens out, and it can be tested.

## The survivors we study

There is a whole genre of business writing that goes: look at these successful companies, here is what they did, do the same and you will succeed. What it almost never does is look for the companies and people who made exactly the same moves and did not make it.

Jerker Denrell put this on a formal footing in 2003. In [Vicarious Learning, Undersampling of Failure, and the Myths of Management](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/vicarious-learning-undersampling-failure-myths-management), he writes that "The organizations that can be observed at any point in time are the survivors of a selective process that has eliminated a large fraction of the underlying population", and that books and the business press then focus on the winners among them. His sharpest finding is about risk: "risky practices, even if they are unrelated to performance in the full population of organizations, may seem to be positively related to performance in a sample of survivors." Bold bets produce spectacular winners and quiet failures, and we only hear about the winners. Which means the question that matters is not what the winners did. It is why the winner was chosen, funded and backed, when others who did the same were not.

## Who is the commodity

Who is the commodity: in music, the songs, the producers, the venues and radio stay scarce while the performer is moved towards commodity; in venture, the investor's network stays scarce while the founder is moved the same way. A sketch of the argument, not a measured map.

One way to say this is with a [Wardley map](why-i-love-wardley-maps.md). On the evolution axis, from genesis to commodity, where does the person sit? Record labels behave like investors: someone with capital chooses which artist to put it behind. And some of the most successful pop acts were not the best musicians, singers or performers. What I used to tell musician friends is: look at who is actually the talent. Often it was not the performer. It was the production, the access to venues and radio, and above all the songs.

The data on songs bears that out. The Pudding found that from 2010 to 2014 [the top ten producers were behind about 40% of the songs](https://pudding.cool/2018/05/similarity/) that reached the top five of the Billboard Hot 100, against about 19% in the late 1980s. In one week of October 2015 only [two songs on the Hot 100 were written by a single person](https://savingcountrymusic.com/the-amount-of-solo-written-hits-has-slipped-from-51-to-2-in-the-last-40-years/), against 51 in 1975. If a label already has the hits packaged, who sings them is, in economic terms, interchangeable. K-pop made it explicit: in 2009 three members of TVXQ [challenged a 13-year contract](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10378131) as unjust, a court granted them an injunction, and the country's fair trade watchdog [set seven years as the maximum](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/20100813/court-invalidates-slave-like-contract-for-entertainers) for standard contracts.

A label that owns the songwriters, the producers and the distribution has moved the performer towards commodity: something to source, standardise and swap. A venture fund can do the same to a founder. There is even a measurement of what investors bring that is not a commodity: when a venture capitalist on a startup's board dies unexpectedly, [startups become more likely to fail and less likely to exit](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f185995/f185995.pdf), while recruitment, product development and CEO replacement are unaffected; Ewens and Sosyura conclude that "a VC's network and reputation are key irreplaceable assets." My reading, and it is mine, is that in the investor's own map the network is scarce and much of the rest can be sourced.

## Why one and not the other

Take two founders with the same opportunity, the same drive, the same idea and the same talent. Sometimes the one who does not get funded even has more advantages. Why does the investor pick the other one?

My hypothesis is that one of the deciding factors is control: which founder the investor believes will take direction, accept decisions made in the investor's interest, and step aside if asked. The investor has no emotional connection to the founder. The founder is there to deliver a return, and is, in the end, replaceable.

Nobody has tested that directly, as far as I could find. But there is a well-studied trait that sits very close to it: coachability. Investors say it openly; Steve Krein of StartUp Health [said](https://medcitynews.com/2012/07/vcs-say-a-coachable-entrepreneur-strong-ip-make-convergence-worth-investing-it/) that one thing he always looks for in an entrepreneur is the question "Are they coachable?" Research by Mitteness and colleagues [showed that coachability influences](https://news.northeastern.edu/faculty-experts/cheryl-mitteness) "whether angel investors recommend moving forward with a company after a pitch", and a study at Babson found that it [has an impact](https://qz.com/858058/startup-investors-value-entrepreneurs-willingness-to-learn-more-than-business-acumen-and-college-degrees) regardless of how investors rank the business's fundamentals or the team's competence.

The key line comes from the researchers themselves. Reviewing the evidence in 2023, [Letwin, Ciuchta, Stevenson and McMahon](https://familybusiness.org/content/being-coachable-can-pay-off-for-founders-up-to-a-point) wrote: "While investors and support organizations such as accelerators prefer coachable entrepreneurs, being coachable doesn't necessarily make your firm more successful." If investors reliably select on a trait that does not reliably predict success, then something other than success is being selected for. Coachability is a polite word for it. Controllability is a less polite one.

## Control is written into the contract

Control moves with performance: when a company performs poorly, investors take full control; as it improves, the founder gains control; when it performs very well, investors keep their cash-flow rights but give up most control. A schematic of Kaplan and Strömberg's finding, not their data.

You do not have to guess whether investors care about control. It is in the paperwork. Kaplan and Strömberg read [213 venture investments in 119 companies](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937X.00245). What distinguishes venture deals, they found, is that cash-flow, voting, board and liquidation rights are separated and allocated separately, often contingent on performance: "if the firm performs poorly, the VCs obtain full control. As performance improves, the entrepreneur retains/obtains more control rights. If the firm performs very well, the VCs retain their cash flow rights, but relinquish most of their control and liquidation rights." That last clause is what I call the big stick: once the founder, or the artist, can credibly say "I leave and you cannot replace me", the contract hands power back.

The same study found it "common for VCs to include non-compete and vesting provisions that make it more expensive for the entrepreneur to leave the firm". Read plainly, they make it costly for a founder to walk away before the investor is ready: if they go, they leave unvested equity behind and cannot compete. Replaceability is not an accident of venture capital; it is designed in.

## Founders get replaced

And the replaceability gets used. Noam Wasserman's study of 212 US startups, reported in [The Founder's Dilemma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18314638/) in 2008, found that by year three "50% of founders were no longer the CEO; in year four, only 40% were still in the corner office; and fewer than 25% led their companies' initial public offerings." He also found that most founders [are shocked when investors insist](https://venturebeat.com/business/founders-what-to-do-when-youre-replaced-as-ceo) they give up control, and that in only about a quarter of replacements did the founder start the change.

The [Ewens and Marx dataset](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2017/11/28/founder-replacement-and-startup-performance/) of 11,929 venture-backed firms is a more conservative count: "Some 15% of the 11,929 firms have at least one founder replacement in our sample period." But the direction is the same. I know many stories of investors with outsized power even at angel, Series A and Series B. The data says those stories are not exceptions.

## Luck, place and timing

None of this says talent and hard work do not matter. They do. So do the right people, the right moment, the right technology and the right place, and two of those are measurable.

Timing and momentum first. In the [MusicLab experiment](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1121066), Salganik, Dodds and Watts had 14,341 people download unknown songs, some seeing what others had downloaded and some not. Showing popularity made success more unequal and less predictable: "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible." That is why copying the winners' moves does not reliably reproduce their results.

Then place. Innovation comes in geographic bursts, because the same people are talking to each other about the same things at the same moment. Enrico Moretti found that [the ten largest clusters in computer science account for 69% of US inventors](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20191277) in the field, that inventors who move into a large cluster produce more, and that when Kodak declined, the productivity of Rochester's other inventors fell too. Being in the room is an advantage the success stories rarely list.

## The investors' answer, and the long run

Investors have a serious reply, and the data partly backs it. Wasserman's later study of [6,130 US startups](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2478) found that "each additional level of founder control (i.e., controlling the board and/or the CEO position) reduces the pre-money valuation of the startup by 17.1–22.0 percent." Ewens and Marx used changes in state non-compete laws to argue that [replacing founders improves performance](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhx130) and makes a high-quality exit more likely. On that reading, investors want control because control creates value.

I accept the numbers. What I do not accept is that they settle the question, because both measure value as the investor measures it: the valuation, the exit. Over a longer run the picture changes. Bain found that [from 1990 to 2014](https://media.bain.com/Images/CHART_DIGEST_Barriers_and_Pathways_to_Sustainable_Growth.pdf), founder-led companies in the S&P 500 "performed 3.1 times better" than the others in shareholder returns, and 1.8 times better excluding technology; [its update this year](https://www.bain.com/insights/the-magic-of-founder-led-companies-snap-chart/) puts the gap since 2015 at 2.1 times. And some of the most valuable companies in the world kept their founders in control on purpose: in April 2026 Mark Zuckerberg held [60.8% of Meta's voting power](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026025532/meta-20260416.htm), and Larry Page and Sergey Brin [about 53% of Alphabet's](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001652044/000130817926000342/goog-20260424.htm), through shares with ten votes each. That is the big stick, written into the share structure.

And there is a selection effect hiding inside the investors' evidence. If investors fund the coachable and replaceable founders in the first place, then the founders in these datasets are already the ones who accepted the terms. The strong-minded founders who walked away, bootstrapped or never got funded are mostly not in the sample. That is Denrell's undersampling again, one level up.

## How to test it

The hypothesis can be tested, and I would like to see it done. Three ways:

1. **Follow the ratings.** Take founders whose coachability investors rated at the pitch, as several studies already did, and follow them for ten years: who was funded, who was replaced, and what happened to the company and its users, not only to the exit.
2. **Compare the mission, not the multiple.** For founder-replaced and founder-kept companies, measure what users experienced after the change: the product's direction, prices, the ethics commitments made at founding, against what the investors' studies measure.
3. **Count the ones who walked away.** Survey founders who were offered term sheets and refused them, and compare them with those who signed. If the refusers score higher on independence and lower on coachability, the filter is real.

## What this means for strong-minded founders

If the hypothesis holds, a particular profile is at a disadvantage in early-stage funding: the founder with a very strong vision, a very clear direction of travel and, sometimes, very strong ethics. Not because they are worse at building companies, but because an investor reads them, correctly, as someone who will not easily be overruled, and who will not make decisions in the investor's interest against the interest of the mission.

That is close to how I would describe many European founders, and it connects to [The expert founders](the-expert-founders.md): the people who know the problem best are often the hardest to steer, because they know better. Tech is the partial exception: winner-takes-all markets mean investors will sometimes accept a founder they cannot control, because the upside of the rare winner pays for everything. But at angel, seed and Series A, before the big stick exists, the founder is negotiating from the weak end of the contract.

The practical conclusion is not to avoid investors. It is to get to the big stick before you need their money, so that the choice of investor is yours. With agents making execution cheap, that is more possible now than it has ever been. What a founder can do with that leverage is the subject of the next piece, [Sell, don't issue](sell-dont-issue.md); what protects each side once control is out of the contract is the subject of the third, [The fork next door](the-fork-next-door.md).

## What this does not claim

- **No study tests "controllability" directly.** Coachability is the nearest proxy; the link from one to the other is my argument.
- **The value evidence cuts against me.** Wasserman and Ewens and Marx find that less founder control and founder replacement go with higher valuations and better exits.
- **Bain's figures are its own analysis.** Not peer-reviewed, and "founder-led" includes founders who stayed on the board.
- **The founder-tenure figures are old.** Wasserman's sample covers startups from the late 1990s and early 2000s.
- **The music figures are mostly industry analyses**, not peer-reviewed studies.
- **Not that investors are villains.** Their incentive is a return; selecting for control follows from that.
- **Not that strong founders always win.** Some are hard to work with for reasons that have nothing to do with ethics.
- **I have a stake.** I am a founder, and this argument suits founders like me.
