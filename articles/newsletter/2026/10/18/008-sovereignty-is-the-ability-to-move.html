Sovereignty is the ability to move: take away the dot, move the cloud and not the code, and focus on the place of highest leverage

By Dinis Cruz, written with the Journalist

Abstract: What does sovereignty mean in practice, for a company, for a small country and for one person choosing what to work on? Seven articles from 12 October, most of them written around a day at a sovereignty event, with Wardley maps underneath. Sovereignty is the ability to move: take each dot you do not control off the map, follow the blast radius, and keep a twin that works for the minimum viable company, tested in QA. Workloads do not move because moving means changing code, so the code must not know, and the reason to move is never sovereignty but value that lands the day you switch. A small country should not enter the race for GPUs; it should build the plumbing, open source, like a road. Open source is a copyright licence whose point is that nobody can lock you in. And the maps themselves: how to read one, where the market is, and how the same rule, the place of highest leverage, chose the work of the week that made all of this possible.

What does sovereignty mean in practice? On 12 October I spent the day at a digital sovereignty event, and most of the conversation was about things Europe does not control and will not control soon: frontier models, giant data centres, GPUs. These seven articles, written that day and the next, argue for the other end of the map, and they use the same tool throughout, the Wardley map, to say where to stand. It follows issue 7, who answers for an agent.

Take away the dot

Sovereignty is the ability to move starts on a padel court. A good player always seems to have time, because they read the ball and move to where it will be; frontier models and GPUs are where the ball is today. Where it is going is the ability to move a workload when you decide, and very few companies can, because everything was clicked into existence and never deployed twice. The test is a Wardley map and one question: take away the dot, what happens? The fix is not an alternative as good as today, which is why most efforts never start. It is a twin that works for the minimum viable company, run in QA every week:

The bar should be: it works.

The code must not know

Move the cloud, not the code comes from a conversation at the same event with the chief executive of an infrastructure company with a large footprint in Romania. What stops workloads moving is not policy or price but code: every call to S3, EC2 and IAM is written into applications nobody wants to touch. So the application must not know it has moved; its calls land on a twin, an open source service the client cannot tell from the real one. The S3 twin exists; the others do not yet. A hyperscaler's own sovereign region is a real step, and it makes the point:

Sovereignty that depends on one company's API is sovereignty by permission.

The part I think matters most is the reason to move, which is never sovereignty or cost, because both are important and never urgent. It is value that lands the day you switch and gives the person signing it cover: backups that exist, versions, encryption with the key held elsewhere, a journal of every call. And no new features during the move.

Judo for small countries

Judo for small countries is written for the people from Romania I met that day and the people in Portugal I talk to every week. The race for GPUs is the commodity end of the map, and a small country should not enter it. The opportunity is the plumbing a talented person in Braga or Cluj needs to sell a service to the world: forming a company, hiring across a border, being paid across Europe, working in every language. Estonia built that road, with X-Road released as open source, and the article's line on why the road has to be open is the one I would put on a ministry's wall:

A road with a toll gate owned by one company is not infrastructure; it is lock-in with tarmac.

What open source is, and what it is not answers misconceptions heard at the event. Open source is a copyright licence, which began as copyleft; a few maintainers decide what goes in, so the control is real and visible; security is about whether somebody had the time to review it; open weights are a compiled binary; and no licence means all rights reserved. Above all, it is not free:

Somebody is always paying.

A map, read as a story

Why I love Wardley maps collects the best of the maps drawn on this site, and the reasons the technique matters: what is moving the needle, the altitude you are optimising at, build or buy, and whether a market exists at all, read for sgit and for RiskMandate. A map is a story, read one frame at a time, and every node is a map of its own. And every dot on a map is a claim:

A map you cannot be wrong about is a diagram.

Where to stand

Places of highest leverage is the same rule for one person with a team of agents that can build in every direction: work where you can add the most value now, are not blocked, and can start. Three days of real steps show it, from the reading meter to the vault, with two walked back and one branch parked. The path looks obvious backwards and was five choices at every step forwards, as long as each step was shipped and used before the next was chosen:

That is the discipline that replaces foresight.

Not a straight line is the case. A day spent improving the newsroom's workflow was, I would argue, a day of work for RiskMandate, because the newsroom had the momentum to solve RiskMandate's oldest problem, where to keep a master vault's key, in two days after six months of attempts. The Cyber Boardroom and MyFeeds paused, and what they taught is in everything the newsroom does, because it was written down and open:

Focus, for me, is undivided attention on the place of highest leverage.
