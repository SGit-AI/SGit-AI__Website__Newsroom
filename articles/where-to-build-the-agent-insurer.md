Three homes for three jobs: underwrite in London, sell into the US and EU, carry the risk in Bermuda.

## In short

- **The company does three jobs, so it can have three homes.** Pricing and selling, distribution, and carrying the risk need different licences and different money.
- **Start as a managing agent in London.** Two of today's AI agent covers are written by Lloyd's coverholders; authorisation takes up to nine months; the intermediary's own capital is small.
- **Sell into the US through surplus lines.** A $143.3 billion market in 2025, built for risks no filed policy covers yet.
- **Reach the EU by one registration,** once software becomes a product there on 9 December 2026; EIOPA is wary of MGAs, so expect questions.
- **Carry the risk in Bermuda when the record allows.** A $1 million floor for a small commercial insurer, an innovative class and a sandbox.
- **Raise capital in the Gulf before selling there.** Saudi insurers need SAR 300 million paid up; the region's funds are the opening.
- **About £4.35 million to start.** Two years as a managing agent, in a model with every assumption stated.
- **Then the capital follows from the limits sold.** £4.8 million to £9.6 million for 2,000 agents; £24 million to £48 million for 10,000.

## The question, and why it has more than one answer

In this series I described [insuring an agent row by row](a-payout-for-every-row.md), [who could carry that risk](who-carries-the-risk.md), and [the vendor's share of it](the-vendors-layer.md). The second article ended with a path: start as a managing agent on someone else's capital, prove the loss record, then carry the risk. So the natural next question is where to build such a company, from a regulatory and an investment point of view: the US, the UK, the EU, or the Middle East, where there are new regimes and new money.

I think the honest answer is that it has more than one home, because it does more than one job. A managing agent prices and sells policies on an insurer's behalf and carries no risk on its own balance sheet; it needs a licence to distribute insurance and a carrier willing to lend its capital. An insurer pays the claims and has to hold capital against a bad year. Distribution needs licences where the customers are. Those three jobs are regulated differently in each place, and the best place for one is not the best place for the others.

The UK, US, EU, Bermuda and the Middle East: what each asks of a managing agent and an insurer.

## Start in London

The first job is pricing and selling, and London is where I would start. The AI agent cover on the market today is written in a handful of ways, and two of them, Armilla and Testudo, are Lloyd's coverholders with Lloyd's syndicates behind them, as [who carries the risk](who-carries-the-risk.md#landscape) set out. A coverholder needs a Lloyd's broker or managing agent to sponsor it, and the market is used to new specialty risks: the Lloyd's Lab's current cohort includes a "Risk engine quantifying advanced and systemic AI risks" ([FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/03/20/lloyds-lab-unveils-cohort-16-insurtech-innovators/), 20 March 2026).

The UK licence is within reach of a start-up. A managing agent is either authorised directly by the FCA, which "can typically take up to nine months from when an applicant starts preparing its application", or works as an appointed representative of a firm that is; its own capital requirement, if it does not hold client money, is the higher of "GBP5,000 or 2.5% of annual income from regulated insurance distribution activity", with professional indemnity cover on top ([DLA Piper](https://intelligence.dlapiper.com/managing-general-agents/?c=GB), September 2026). For the later step, the PRA has run a mobilisation regime for new insurers since 31 December 2024, "to facilitate new insurer expansion and entry into the UK market" ([Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/prudential-regulation/new-insurer-start-up-unit)), with a minimum capital floor of £2.4 million for a general insurer.

## Sell into the US

The US is the largest market, and the way a new risk reaches it is surplus lines. They cover "unique risks for which admitted carriers do not offer a filed policy form or rate", and the industry's own association describes new products with no loss history as "difficult, if not impossible, to price or rate for insurance purposes" ([WSIA](https://www.wsia.org/wcm/wcm/About/What_is_Surplus_Lines.aspx)). That is exactly an agent behaviour policy today. The market is large and growing: "The surplus lines market closed 2025 at a record $143.3 billion in direct premium written" ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/excess-surplus/surplus-lines-hit-143bn-record-as-placement-market-shifts-589475.aspx), citing AM Best, 11 September 2026). Harbor's AI agent cover is issued by surplus lines carriers.

A US managing agent needs a producer licence in each state it writes in, and the NAIC's model law asks for a bond of "at least $100,000 or ten percent (10%) of the managing general agent's total annual written premium ... but in no event greater than $500,000" ([NAIC](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/model-law-225.pdf)). The US MGA market reached "approximately $128 billion in 2025", and "approximately 20% of total US MGA premium is supported through fronting carrier relationships" ([Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/conning-estimates-us-mga-premiums-reached-128bn-in-2025/), citing Conning, 29 July 2026). So a London managing agent can reach US customers through the surplus lines market, with a US entity when the book justifies one.

The US also has the cheapest way for a provider to insure its own agents: a captive. In Vermont, a pure captive needs capital of "not less than $250,000.00" ([Vermont statutes](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/08/141/06004)). The providers who, as [who carries the risk](who-carries-the-risk.md#providers) found, have looked at insuring their own risk could start there.

## The EU, second

The EU is the market where demand may arrive fastest, because from 9 December 2026, as [the vendor's layer](the-vendors-layer.md#europe) described, software is a product under the new product liability directive. Distribution is simple in principle: an intermediary registered in one member state works across the union on "the principle of a single registration" ([Central Bank of Ireland](https://edit.centralbank.ie/regulation/industry-market-sectors/brokers-retail-intermediaries/insurance-brokers-intermediaries/passporting-in-and-out)). An insurer's minimum capital under Solvency II starts at €2.7 million.

The supervisor is cautious about exactly this model. EIOPA wrote in June 2026 that "insurers are increasingly outsourcing key functions to MGAs, creating concerns about weak oversight, conflicts of interests, unclear responsibility for product governance and difficulties in cross-border supervision", and that it "could engage with the Lloyd's market to better understand how we can build capacity in the EU" ([EIOPA](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/document/download/e4de2f84-0a0a-42ed-82b6-b8b5620c7745_en?filename=2026-06-17+-+EIOPA+Note+on+Competitiveness.pdf)). A company whose product is a written, deterministic policy with clear governance should answer those concerns better than most; I would still plan the EU as the second market, not the first home.

## Carry the risk in Bermuda

When the loss record exists and the company is ready to carry risk, I would do it in Bermuda. Its regime has a class for this stage: a Class 3A "Small commercial insurer" holds capital set by a risk-based formula, with "a $1 million floor" ([BMA](https://bma.bm/insurance-licensing)). It has a class for an insurer that "intends to carry on general business in an innovative manner", and a sandbox for "a company aiming to carry on general business in an innovative and experimental manner"; cyber insurers have used both, and one, Breach Insurance, moved from the sandbox to the innovative class in 2023 ([BMA](https://www.bma.bm/insurance-innovation)). And it is where risk capital meets insurance: the Bermuda Stock Exchange listed "more than 93% of global issuance" of insurance-linked securities in 2025, including "new cyber catastrophe bonds" ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/reinsurance/news/breaking-news/bermuda-stock-exchange-seizes-93-of-ils-market-as-cat-bond-issuance-explodes-564989.aspx), 11 February 2026). Once agent risk has a record, that is the market that will take its tail.

The UK is the alternative, with the PRA's mobilisation regime and the same Solvency-style discipline; and an insurer that wants EU clients directly may need an EU carrier as well. But for a first balance sheet, Bermuda's floor and its capital markets make it the place I would look first.

## The Middle East, for capital first

The Gulf has new regimes and a great deal of money, and I think its first role is the money. As an insurance market, its entry costs differ widely: in Saudi Arabia an insurer's paid-up capital "shall not be less than three hundred million Saudi riyals" under the law as amended in 2021; in Abu Dhabi, the ADGM's captive rules started from US$150,000, and its regulator proposed in December 2025 that insurers hold "120% of the ASCR under normal circumstances" ([Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/fsra-proposes-comprehensive-updates-to-adgm-insurance-regulatory-framework/)). We could not read the DIFC regulator's own pages, so its figures are from secondary sources and are left out here.

As a source of capital it is a different story: the region's funds invest in balance sheets of exactly the kind the third stage needs, and the ADGM's regulator is rewriting its insurance rules as it does so. I would raise there, and sell there later.

## How much it would take

You asked for an opinion on the investment, so here is mine, with the arithmetic behind it in [a data file](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/articles/data/where-to-build-the-agent-insurer__capital.json) and every assumption stated in the script that writes it.

**Stage one, two years as a managing agent: about £4.35 million.** A team of eight in the first year and fifteen in the second, at £150,000 each including everything, and £450,000 a year for regulatory, legal, actuarial, professional indemnity and technology costs: £1.65 million in the first year and £2.7 million in the second. No capital backs the policies, because the carrier's does. That is a seed or Series A, and the market is funding it: insurtech funding was "US$5.08 billion" in 2025 ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2026/03/09/860638.htm), 9 March 2026, citing Gallagher Re), and in the first quarter of 2026 "InsurTechs linked to AI liability and cyber insurance attracted USD 444.84 million" ([Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/global-insurtech-funding-holds-firm-as-ai-dominates-q126-investment-gallagher-re/), 7 May 2026). AIUC has raised $55 million in two rounds, and Harbor was reported this month to have raised a $10 million seed.

**Stage two, carrying the risk: the capital follows from the limits sold.** This is where the deterministic policy pays off. Because every row is capped and every unnamed capability excluded, the worst year can be bounded from the limits on the book: take all the limits sold, the share of them one shared failure could hit at once (one model's flaw, one tool's bug, every agent built on it), keep half after reinsurance, and add a 20% buffer.

Capital to carry the risk, from the limits sold and the share one shared failure could hit. Illustrative.

With £80,000 of limits per agent, as in the worked example:

| Agents | Limits on the book | 5% hit at once | 10% hit at once |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | £40 million | £1.2 million (the UK floor makes it £2.4 million) | £2.4 million |
| 2,000 | £160 million | £4.8 million | £9.6 million |
| 10,000 | £800 million | £24 million | £48 million |

So my opinion: raise about £5 million to start, which is stage one with some headroom; plan a second raise of £5 million to £10 million to carry the first two thousand agents, from investors who fund balance sheets; and by ten thousand agents, the capital is a reinsurance and capital markets question, which is where Bermuda comes in. The share hit at once is the number that matters most, and it is the one with no loss data behind it yet: it is why the first books should be small and spread across models and tools.

## What this does not claim

- **This is not legal, tax or regulatory advice.** It maps what regulators publish; a real decision needs lawyers and regulators in each place.
- **The model's inputs are mine.** Team sizes, costs, limits, the shared-failure share and the reinsured half are assumptions chosen to show the shape; the capital a regulator would ask for comes from its own formula.
- **Currencies are approximate.** The floors are as each regulator states them; the model converts them to pounds roughly.
- **The Middle East figures are thinner.** The DIFC's pages could not be read; the ADGM captive figures are from its 2015 rules; Saudi Arabia's guidelines and its law give different minimums, and the law's SAR 300 million is used.
- **Fronting fees are left out.** We found no reliable published figure for what a carrier charges to lend its licence and balance sheet.
- **Tax is left out.** Bermuda's 15% corporate income tax from 2025 applies only to groups with revenue of €750 million or more.
