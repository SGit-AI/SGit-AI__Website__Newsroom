Local trust, certified upwards: why local and niche publishers should take everything that has become commodity, certify the big outlets' stories about their patch and be paid for it, and why stories, like software, need someone to maintain them

By Dinis Cruz · 2026-10-25 · article v1.1.0, 2 versions · newsroom v0.13.76 · newslocal-newsbusiness-modelstrustprovenanceopen-bankingarticle

Abstract: The companion to my reply to Bert Kok. The publishers with the most to gain from AI are not the big generic ones but the local and the niche: the ones close to their readers, in their language and culture, with real expertise. They own the reader's trust, and trust is where the value is now. Such a publisher can take everything that has become commodity, the weather, sport results from official sources, the big outlets' global news, and serve it to its readers from their point of view, captured and kept current by agents. And it can certify upwards: when a big outlet runs a story about its area, the local journalists are the ones who can say whether it is right, and should be paid for saying so. A story with no local source behind it reads as a shadow, and readers will go to the source. This needs open interfaces, as the Competition and Markets Authority forced UK banks to open theirs from 2018, and it needs a map of who reports and who recycles, where a newsroom that cuts its experts shows it. Stories, like software, go off if nobody maintains them, and the same argument I made for maintainers applies to journalists: whoever does the expert work should be the one who is paid and the one who signs off.

In short

  • The value is in the local and the niche. Close to the reader, in their language and culture, with expertise.
  • They own the trust. And trust is what is worth paying for now.
  • Commodity flows in. Weather, sport results, global news, served from the reader's point of view.
  • Certification flows up. Local journalists vouch for the big outlets' stories about their area, and are paid.
  • A story without a local source is a shadow. Readers can go to the source.
  • Interfaces must open, as UK banks' did under the Competition and Markets Authority.
  • Map who reports and who recycles. A newsroom that cuts its experts should show it.
  • Stories go off like milk. Someone has to maintain them, as maintainers maintain software.

Where the value is

I think what is happening is that, alongside the very big, very generic news organisations, we are going to see more very focused publishers: local, regional, or expert in one subject. That is where the real value is, where you can charge, and where it makes sense to invest in journalism and in creating content. Because that is where the trust is.

A local or niche publisher is close to its readers. It works in their language, their culture, their region. Its business model tends to stay aligned with its readers' needs, because its readers are all it has. The bigger an organisation gets, the more business models it runs, and the more easily it drifts into rent extraction. That can work, and it suits commodity, but it loses the edge, and it loses trust. There is an economic value now in going back to trust, and in selling it. The local and the niche are the ones best placed to do that.

Commodity flows in

A local publisher that owns its readers' trust should not stop at its own reporting. It should have everything that has become commodity on its site, including what comes from other sites.

Some of it is plain fact from official, unambiguous sources: the weather, the sport results, the trains, the markets. Those can be captured and kept current by agents, with their sources attached, at almost no cost. Some of it is the big outlets' global news. That too belongs on the local site, told from the point of view of its readers: what this means here, for us, in our language.

So I go to my local news site, which I trust because it is local, and I get the weather, the latest news, the sport, and the global story of the day, from a place I trust, with its sources shown. The commodity layer becomes a reason to come back, which is the habit Bert Kok rightly says publishers must not lose, without costing the newsroom its time.

Open the interfaces

This only works if the commodity can flow, which means interfaces have to open. There is a precedent. In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority found in 2016 that established banks did not have to compete hard enough, and one of its remedies was open banking: since 2018, customers have been able to share their account data securely with other providers, through a standard built under the CMA's order. Open Banking Limited, the body the CMA's order set up, says there are now over 18 million active users. I had remembered it as the FCA's doing; it was the CMA's.

News has nothing like it yet. The agents on the PHP story site watch 913 pages, and 59 of them answered with a bot challenge and 15 with a paywall. Some of that is right: the publisher should be paid. But a standard way to read, cite and pay for a source, with billing built in, would serve the local publisher, the source and the reader better than walls and challenges.

Certification flows up

Here is the other half. If I read a story about my town on one of the big websites, say the BBC, I would expect its source to be a local outlet I trust, or a local agency that actually employs local journalists, and I would expect those journalists to have certified the story: this is right, or this is right with this caveat. That is the economic model: the big outlet pays the local source for the certification, per use, as I described in signed by the source.

And if a story has no such source behind it, I know what I am reading. It is a shadow of the real reporting, and I can go and read the local one instead.

Who reports, and who recycles

That means mapping who is doing real journalism and who is recycling it. We are starting to have the data for this: the PHP site keeps every source and every capture, so it can see who reported first, who quoted whom, and who changed their words later. A global organisation that recycles content created by others should pay for it. Either it sends its own reporter, and that is fine, or it pays the people who did.

And the cuts should be visible. Budgets for correspondents are cut partly because nobody can see the effect. When a news organisation lets its cybersecurity correspondent go, or the only expert in a subject, it should show on its site and in its trust: the person who was certifying the updates, and maintaining the stories, is not there any more.

Stories go off like milk

That last point matters because stories need maintaining. As I argued in sources and their shadows, a news organisation's history is becoming something like case law: it is referenced, quoted, and built on. But a story with facts that can change goes off, like milk. The number gets revised, the claim gets withdrawn, the source rewrites its post. Somebody with the expertise has to keep the story true, or say that it is no longer current. That is ongoing work, and it is paid work.

The same argument as for software

This is exactly the argument I made about software in who maintains the PHP you run?. The maintainer, the person who knows the code, should be paid, and should sign off the version you run. The journalist, the expert who takes the time to understand something, should be paid, and should sign off the stories that rest on their work. In both cases the free-riders are invisible today, and in both cases the damage shows only when the expert has gone and nobody can fix, or vouch for, what is left. Making who signs for what visible is how both get funded.

What exists, and what is proposed

  • Exists: the capture, provenance and source watch on the PHP site; stories kept as graphs; the bridge story, a simulated local story followed to the end; signed notes and keys on pki.sgit.ai.
  • Proposed: certification paid per use; open, billable interfaces for news; a public map of who reports and who recycles; trust that reflects a newsroom's staffing.

What this does not claim

  • This is an economic model, not a market. No local publisher is paid for certification today.
  • Trust in local news is an argument here, not a measurement in this article.
  • The open banking parallel is about the mechanism, an order that opened interfaces, not a claim that news should be regulated the same way.
  • Not every big outlet recycles. Many still send their own reporters; the point is to make the difference visible.
